DAY 10 · ANTHROPOLOGY OF RITUAL

Ritual and Collective Effervescence

The counter-intuitive line of the day: the most uncomfortable stretch of a ritual is not the filler between the good parts — it is the whole content

2026-09-03 · BigCat
The email announcing your promotion does not make you that person. You become that person when someone says your name out loud in front of a room.

On your first day you are added to a dozen chat groups and a new laptop is waiting on your desk. And you know perfectly well you are still an outsider. The moment you actually feel like one of them comes much later, and it happens somewhere specific — an all-hands where someone mentions your name and the room laughs.

This page takes that kind of occasion apart. It is easy to read as empty formality, but the mechanism is finer than that: the work is done by the stretch in the middle where you belong nowhere; the force people feel in a crowd comes from somewhere they systematically misidentify; and most of the modern occasions that feel intense change nobody's position at all.

// 01

You are no longer the old person, and not yet the new one

You have handed in your resignation and the new job starts next month. Where are you for those two weeks? You are still in the old company's chat groups, but nobody tags you; the new company has not added you yet. You belong nowhere, and most people just want the stretch over with.

In 1909 the Frenchman Arnold van Gennep published Les rites de passage. He was an outsider who never held a proper chair at a French university. What he did was lay initiations, weddings, funerals and installations from all over the world side by side, and find that the skeleton was always the same: separation, liminality, incorporation. First you are prised off your old position, then you are held for a while in a state that is nothing at all, then you are fitted into another position. For that middle stretch he borrowed the Latin word limen, a doorway threshold, which is why it is now called liminality.

The person who made that stretch vivid was Victor Turner. From 1950 to 1954 he did fieldwork among the Ndembu of north-western Zambia, then Northern Rhodesia. One thing he recorded is called Mukanda: boys of a certain age are taken away from their mothers into a camp built in the bush and stay for months. The camp rules are hard — no ordinary clothes, no ordinary names, differences of birth flattened out.

In The Ritual Process (1969) Turner wrote that such people are neither here nor there: not one of the slots society keeps ready will fit them just then. And it is precisely in that slotless time that something grows, which he named communitas — the bare connection left between people once every difference of position has been stripped off. Those boys still recognise each other decades later on the strength of a few months.

The mechanism, plainly: a new identity cannot be loaded into someone who is still occupying the old one, so the old one has to be dismantled first. The discomfort is not a side effect of the transition; the discomfort is the dismantling. You have seen the small version at work — the real change never happens in the email announcing the reorg, it happens in the weeks afterwards when nobody knows who you report to.

Be clear about the evidence: this is a comparative framework plus a set of ethnographic cases, not a falsifiable hypothesis. Van Gennep did no such fieldwork himself; he worked entirely from other people's records. It holds up because a century later it still applies wherever you point it, not because it was ever tested.

At each end are the slots society keeps ready, and the position line is solid. In the middle the line drops and breaks into dashes: marks are stripped off and no slot fits anyone. People who enter together are linked sideways for the duration, and are fitted into a different slot on the way out.
// 02

What appears in a room when everyone does the same thing at the same time

In the second a goal goes in, tens of thousands of people stand up and shout the same syllable. You get goosebumps even if it is not your team. Afterwards it seems slightly absurd — you did not care that much who won.

In 1912 Émile Durkheim published The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life. He had never been to Australia; he worked from Baldwin Spencer and Frank Gillen's 1890s accounts of the Arunta (now usually written Arrernte), especially the all-night gatherings of a whole group. He read these as religion in its earliest form — a premise that no longer stands: the idea that the simplest society shows the earliest stage was an evolutionary assumption of the period, and the material was second-hand besides. What collapsed is the premise; what survived is the mechanism.

He called that mechanism collective effervescence. Ordinarily people live scattered lives; occasionally they assemble and sing, move and shout together, and the temperature in the room goes up. At that point people genuinely feel a force, and feel that it is not theirs — that it is pressing in from outside. Durkheim's decisive step: the force is the assembled group itself, and that is exactly what the people in it cannot perceive. So it gets hung onto something concrete and nearby — a pole, a flag, a logo.

Which yields something you would not otherwise see: sacredness is not a property of the object. It is the residual heat of an assembly, stored in an object. That explains the odd fact that a relic looks unremarkable on an ordinary day and can detonate a crowd the moment it is insulted.

Is there harder modern evidence? One piece. In 2011 Ivana Konvalinka and colleagues published, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, a set of measurements from a fire-walking ritual in the Spanish town of San Pedro Manrique: heart-rate monitors on 12 fire-walkers and 26 spectators, nine of whom were relatives or close friends of a walker, the rest recruited at random hours before the ritual. The related spectators' heart-rate dynamics tracked the walkers'; the unrelated ones did not. Evidence: one site, a very small sample, and no way to randomly assign who is whose relative — it shows that co-presence produces physiological synchrony, not which way the causation runs.

The laboratory side has to be discounted. In 2009 Scott Wiltermuth and Chip Heath had people walk and sing in step and then cooperate more in economic games; in 2010 Schachner and Garvin ran a direct replication of the third of those experiments and did not get it. In 2017 Mogan, Fischer and Bulbulia pooled 42 studies (4,327 people) and put synchrony's effect on prosocial behaviour at around 0.28. So the force is real, but much smaller and much more conditional than a century of people quoting Durkheim have implied.

In 2004 Randall Collins, in Interaction Ritual Chains, broke it into four conditions you can check one at a time: bodies in the same place, a barrier keeping outsiders out, attention locked on the same thing, and a shared mood. With all four, people leave carrying what he calls emotional energy.

// 03

The ones who hurt most gave most — and so did the people watching

In 2013 Dimitris Xygalatas and colleagues published a study from Mauritius in Psychological Science. The local Hindu community keeps the festival of Thaipusam, which includes two rituals: one is collective prayer and singing; the other, the Kavadi, involves piercing the skin with hooks and skewers and dragging weights up to a temple. The researchers worked with 86 men and afterwards invited them to donate anonymously to a local temple.

The result has two layers: Kavadi performers gave more than prayer participants; and people who merely watched the Kavadi also gave more than prayer participants. The higher the self-reported pain, the larger the donation. Evidence: a field quasi-experiment, one site, an all-male sample — trust the direction, not the number as a universal constant.

The mechanism is that a cost settles the matter. Anyone can say they are one of us, and saying it costs nothing; taking on an ordeal that cannot be faked leaves nothing to doubt. Which is why the second layer matters most: the audience is not there to gawk, it is a required part — a cost nobody witnesses has no recipient.

So what are all the intense modern occasions? Turner poured cold water on that himself. In 1982 he proposed a second word, liminoid, for the things in industrial society that have the shape of liminality but not the substance: festivals, marathons, all-night hackathons. Two differences — they are optional, and your position is unchanged when they end; genuine liminality is compulsory, and the person who comes out has been rewritten. The word never caught on, but the distinction is hard: however intense the offsite, if you return to the same job it was not a rite of passage.

Real modern passages are elsewhere: graduations, weddings, courtroom oaths, being introduced to the room on your first day, funerals, a last day at work. What they share is not grandeur but a public declaration, after which other people genuinely treat you differently. Without that step the transition stays suspended — which is often why someone is still asking you to approve things six months after you left.

One side of this has to be stated plainly: a ritual that makes an in-group necessarily makes an out-group. The same mechanism that binds the people in the room also marks out who is not in it — the other face of the boundary-drawing covered in the piece on the power of groups. Functionalists read social integration in these facts; conflict theorists read exclusion and the reproduction of rank in the same facts. The disagreement is not about the observations but about which face counts as the main effect, and that is still contested.

🎒 In practice · BigCat

  • ① Leading teams and organisations—a reorg is announced by email, with the new structure and reporting lines, and the next day everyone holds their usual meetings. This runs the mechanism from "You are no longer the old person, and not yet the new one": separation and incorporation only, the middle stretch skipped, the old identity never dismantled — which is why someone is still updating the old board three months on. What to change: hold one closing meeting and read out the old structure's goals, metrics and owners one by one, declaring each closed, leaving nothing to lapse on its own. The metric to watch is not awareness of the new structure but how many questions are still being asked in the old channels and on the old board in week four.
  • ② Practice and inner work—sitting at home goes fine for two weeks and falls apart after a month; one group retreat and it holds for half a year. This runs the mechanism from "What appears in a room when everyone does the same thing at the same time": co-presence plus shared attention hands you a stock of emotional energy, and that stock decays, which willpower does not top up. What to change: stop scheduling it as "when I have time" and fix the date of the next group session in the calendar before leaving the current one. The metric is not whether you sat today but how many days lie between two group occasions.

🌀 Crossing over · connections beyond the field

  • Evolutionary biology · what the apparently pointless rules are actually measuring: evolutionary biology has a notion called costly signalling, proposed by Amotz Zahavi in 1975. A peacock's tail is heavy and gets in the way, and precisely because it gets in the way it cannot be faked — a bird in poor condition could not carry the cost. The apparently pointless requirements of religious communities run the mechanism from "The ones who hurt most gave most": a cost cannot be faked, so what it says is binding. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach: to predict how long a voluntary organisation will last, how expensive its requirements are tells you more than whether its ideas are right — and the expense has to be of a kind that is visible inside and useless outside. Richard Sosis and Eric Bressler assembled data on 83 nineteenth-century American communes in 2003: the costlier the requirements, the longer the commune lasted, and the relation was much stronger in religious than in secular communes (a historical correlation, not an experiment — do not read it as causation). But it does not hold here: Zahavi's model requires a stable correspondence between the cost of the signal and the quality being measured — the heavier the tail, the more surely only a healthy bird can bear it. There is no such function between ritual pain and whether a person will cooperate later: pain tolerance is physiological variation and does not have to run in the same direction as willingness to cooperate. So what ritual screens out is people unwilling to pay, not people unable to — one and the same thing for the peacock, two different things for us.
  • Distributed systems and engineering · nobody crosses the line until everyone reaches it: parallel computing has a basic primitive called a barrier — a set of tasks running at once must stop at a certain point and wait until the last one arrives before any of them enters the next phase. This runs the mechanism from "You are no longer the old person, and not yet the new one": the function of the liminal stretch is to keep a batch of people in the same state of being nothing until they can leave together — the Ndembu bush camp taking a cohort in and letting it out together is not a coincidence. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach: a transition a batch goes through together and an equally long one a person goes through alone do not produce the same thing — the first grows lateral ties across the cohort as well, the second only produces a vertical change of status. So moving onboarding, training or rotations from fixed cohorts to rolling admission improves scheduling, but the cohort layer is structurally cancelled, and no amount of team-building afterwards restores it. But it does not hold here: the cost of a barrier in engineering is the waiting, since the slowest task sets everyone's pace, which is why engineers work hard to eliminate it. The liminal stretch is the reverse: the waiting is the output. The task before and after a barrier is the same task; the person before and after liminality is not. Same shape — one wants the wait as short as possible, the other stops working when the wait gets short.

Questions worth sitting with

An online graduation, an online memorial — which ingredient is missing?
Check Collins's four: shared attention is there, shared mood just about (a comment stream shows you others crying too), and a barrier can be built. What is missing is bodies in the same place — you cannot feel the breathing of the person beside you, or confirm that anyone else is feeling the same thing. So what works online is the declaration; what does not is the effervescence.
Is a ritual nobody believes in any more still doing something?
Probably yes, in a different way. Collective effervescence does not require you to believe the content, only bodies present, attention aligned and mood shared — a family new year dinner or a season-opening ceremony works on exactly that. What kills a ritual is not disbelief but nobody turning up, or everyone turning up and looking at their phones. So how many people actually come, and whether their attention really is on the same thing, predicts how much of it still works better than how many people endorse it.
How do you tell collective effervescence apart from being manipulated?
Not by the mechanism — the same conditions produce the ecstasy in a stadium and other things too, and the mechanism is indifferent to the use. What separates them is elsewhere: whether entry and exit are free, whether you can criticise it publicly afterwards without losing standing, and whether anything checkable is left once the crowd disperses. The judgement rests on those observable things, not on how genuine the feeling was at the time — the feeling is equally genuine either way.
