DAY 4 · SOCIOLOGICAL THEORY

Marx's Sociological Legacy

Many of his predictions ran the wrong way; the analytical tools he left behind are still used by everyone

2026-08-28 · BigCat
The boys who first saw through the promise that schooling would change their lives were, precisely because they saw through it, the first to walk into the factory.

End of the day, someone asks what you did. Seven or eight things passed through your hands, and you cannot say which of them was yours. You are not exactly tired. You are empty.

The three tools on this page all start from that feeling: why work can hollow a person out, what actually divides positions in a society, and how one particular account gets to become common sense. One line drawn up front — this page deals only with the tools themselves and the empirical tests they have since been put through, not with political claims. An explanation that says clearly what will happen is exactly an explanation that cannot say what ought to be wanted.

// 01

What you make stops being yours the moment you finish it

In 1844 a twenty-six-year-old in Paris wrote a stack of manuscripts. He never published them; they sat for nearly ninety years and were only printed in the early 1930s — which is why the "young Marx" twentieth-century readers met arrived more than half a century after Capital.

The manuscripts contain a word that is easy to misread: alienation (Entfremdung, sometimes rendered as estrangement). It is not "the work is exhausting" and not "I dislike this job". It names a set of severed relations. Marx listed four.

First, to the product: you make the thing, and when it is finished it belongs to someone else, and the power it accumulates presses back down on you. Second, to the act of producing: what gets made, how, and when you stop are none of them yours to decide, so it is precisely while working that you are least yourself, and only after hours that you feel alive. Third, to the part of us that is distinctly human (he called it species-being, Gattungswesen): what separates people from other animals is that we can draw the plan in our heads before our hands move; once labour is nothing but a means of getting fed, that is stripped out. Fourth, to other people: those working on the same thing become each other's competitors.

Four severed relations. Not one of them is "how hard the work is" — the driving variable in this account is always control and ownership.

In 1964 Blauner rewrote the four into questions you can actually ask — powerlessness, meaninglessness, isolation, self-estrangement — and surveyed factories under four very different technologies: printing (a craft), textiles (tending machines), automobile assembly lines, and chemicals (continuous process). The result was not "the more advanced the technology, the more alienated the worker" but an inverted U: craft workers lowest, assembly highest, and chemicals dropping back down again — because there the workers watch over a whole system on the strength of specialist knowledge, and so actually hold discretion.

Pin the evidence down: a 1960s cross-sectional survey plus industry case studies, not a causal design. The criticism it draws is sharp too — by treating technology as the prime mover, Blauner set aside the question of who owns the thing, which is the core of Marx's account. So this is a two-edged result: it supports autonomy as the key variable while pushing the ownership explanation off to the side.

In 1983 Hochschild saw another layer at an airline's flight-attendant training centre: trainees were told over and over to treat the cabin as their own living room and each stranger as a personal guest, and to make the smile look real. She called this emotional labour, and separated surface acting (changing only the expression) from deep acting (actually working the feeling up). One more item goes on the alienation list: what is bought is not only your time and your hands but what your feelings are supposed to be. The evidence is observation of training plus interviews — a case study.

// 02

The question is not how much you earn, it is where the money comes from

Two people took in the same amount this month: one a salary, one the rent on two flats. On the income table they stand side by side; but if both stopped what they were doing, next month would pull them apart at once.

Class, for Marx, is not a rung on an income ladder. It is your relation to the means of production — whether you hold something that lets other people work for you (premises, equipment, capital) or have only your own labour power to sell. Two consequences follow. First, class is relational: one side's position is defined by the other, unlike "annual income", which is a property of each person separately. Second, the two sides' interests in distribution systematically fail to coincide, so tension is built into the order without anyone having to be malicious. That is the skeleton of conflict theory.

The other camp's strongest version belongs here too. In 1945 Davis and Moore gave the functionalist account: stratification is an allocation machine — some positions are harder, more consequential, and take longer to train for, so society has to attach higher pay and prestige to them to fill them at all. Tumin's 1953 reply is just as strong: who decides which position is "more consequential"? And stratification itself blocks a set of capable people from ever getting a chance to try. The debate ran for more than twenty years and was never settled; neither side should be used as a finding.

It also has to be said honestly that several of his main predictions pointed the wrong way. The middle strata were not squeezed out of existence — they expanded enormously across the twentieth century. Workers did not grow poorer in absolute terms — real wages in the developed industrial countries rose over the long run. And revolution did not come first where industry was deepest; it came in agrarian countries.

The repairs happened inside the tradition as well. In the 1970s Erik Olin Wright coined "contradictory class locations": a middle manager neither owns the firm nor merely sells labour, yet really does direct other people's work, and so occupies part of two positions at once. Empirical research today more often uses a different schema (known by its three authors' surnames, Erikson–Goldthorpe–Portocarero): rather than a two-way split on ownership, it divides by the type of employment relation — at one end a labour contract paid by the hour or the piece, at the other the "service relationship" offered to professional and managerial posts: salary, career ladder, long-term commitment, traded for loyalty and discretion.

Strength of evidence: such schemas hold stable correlations with health, education and voting, but they overlap heavily with income and credentials, so it is hard to say which is doing the work; and recent research questions whether a scheme built on industrial-era employment forms still fits.

// 03

Whether an account catches on and whether it is true are two different things

During a reorganisation, the phrase "we're all one family" suddenly gets said a lot more often. Not necessarily by anyone performing. The interesting part is: why that phrase, why at that moment, out of so many mouths at once.

Marx offered a famous metaphor in 1859: a society has an economic base (how people produce, and what relations they enter into while producing), with a superstructure of law, politics and ideas standing on it. The criticism goes first: the strict version is too mechanical, the arrow points one way only. That is not just an outsider's objection — Engels himself stepped back late in life, writing that the economic is determining only "in the last instance". Almost nobody uses the strict version today.

What has lasted is the other claim. The German Ideology puts it as: the ruling ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class. Read it as a mechanism rather than a conspiracy — not that anyone is lying, but that some accounts have the conditions to be mass-produced, repeated, and finally taken as obvious, which has to do with who holds the presses, the schools, the studios, the recommendation slots. Whether an account spreads and whether it is true run along two different causal chains, and there is no reason to assume they always coincide.

The fieldwork that shows this mechanism best reaches a counter-intuitive conclusion. In the early 1970s Paul Willis followed twelve working-class boys at a boys' school in the English Midlands, observing in school for a year and a half and then following them into work for six months. These boys were not fooled by the school. They saw through the promise that studying hard would lift them out: the places available were few, and the price was a decade of compliance. Having seen through it, they chose resistance — disrupting lessons, looking down on the boys who worked, holding manual masculinity high. And it was exactly that repertoire that walked them into manual jobs. Willis's conclusion: putting people back where they started does not require deception. Partial insight can do it too.

Nail the evidence down: a case ethnography of twelve people — one school, all boys, an English industrial town fifty years ago — which cannot be extrapolated into a general law. Common criticisms include that he romanticises the counter-culture and treats the sexism and violence inside it too lightly.

The opposite hand belongs on the table as well. The Weber and Rationalization entry runs the other way: ideas are not derivative, and an anxiety about salvation was able to produce an entire economic discipline. Both together are the standard practice today — which weighs more in which setting is an empirical question, not a question of allegiance.

🎒 In Practice · BigCat

  • ① Leading teams and organisations — a situation that recurs: something ships, the people who built it are moved onto the next thing the same day, and nobody ever comes back to say how it went. The section "What you make stops being yours the moment you finish it" locates what is cut here: the link between "I made this" and "I see what became of it". It has nothing to do with pay, and money will not restore it. The change is small: write a follow-up date into the plan at delivery — three months out, send the usage picture back to the people who built it, stated explicitly as not for evaluation — and retire the default that shipping ends the project.
  • ② Health and energy — the same ten hours: on some days you still want to read something in the evening, on others you get home and can only collapse, and the two days looked about equally hard. The difference is often not the length but how many of those ten hours you sequenced yourself. What to change is the metric you record: not hours worked, but "how many hours today did I set the order of". Keep it for two weeks, then see whether it tracks with having anything left in the evening.

🌀 Crossings · Interdisciplinary Leaps

  • Neuroscience · agency is perception, not judgement: there is a robust lab finding called intentional binding — press a button yourself and then hear a tone, and you judge the gap between them as shorter than it was; have the same finger moved for you and hear the same tone, and the compression disappears. In other words, "I did that" is not a conclusion reached afterwards; it has already altered perception itself. The mechanism in "What you make stops being yours the moment you finish it" says the key variable is control, not how strenuous the work is. From this follows a conclusion you could not otherwise see: for the same piece of work, "assigned" and "chosen" are not two descriptions of one thing — they have already diverged at the lowest level of perception; so treating autonomy as a perk exchangeable for a raise does not work mechanically — pay acts on whether you are willing to do it, control acts on what doing it is like, and these are different layers. But it does not hold here: intentional binding is a millisecond-scale, within-person effect that can be manipulated at will in a lab; alienation is about institutional relations between people — who decides what gets made, who the output belongs to, what you are to your colleagues. You can perfectly well manufacture some sense of agency by letting people order their own tasks while ownership and distribution do not move at all — and not one of the four relations has changed. Taking alienation for a scaled-up sense of agency quietly reduces the whole analysis to an interface design problem.
  • Evolutionary biology · a tug-of-war needs no one to be unreasonable: in 1974 Trivers pointed out that parents and offspring have overlapping but not identical genetic interests, so conflicts over weaning and food are to be expected without assuming anyone is being difficult. Two decades later Haig extended the same logic into pregnancy: genes acting on the fetal side are selected to draw more nutrient transfer, genes on the maternal side to hold transfer at the mother's own optimum, and gestational diabetes and pre-eclampsia are on this reading by-products of that pull. The mechanism in "The question is not how much you earn, it is where the money comes from" has exactly this shape: conflict comes from a mismatch of interests set by position, while both sides must go on cooperating. From this follows a conclusion you could not otherwise see: where the same tug-of-war recurs in a long-running cooperative relationship, its intensity is predictable from how far the interests overlap, and is largely independent of the parties' personalities and communication quality; so what to change in order to damp it is the distribution rule, not the number of meetings. But it does not hold here: "interest" in evolution is genetic relatedness, a number you can compute and the parties cannot alter; a social position is a historically made arrangement, it can change, and people hold interpretations of and identifications with their own position — they can even stand against it (Marx himself was not a worker). Treating social conflict as a constant of the kind genetic conflict is happens to be precisely what this tradition most objects to.

Going Deeper

If the main predictions failed, why is the analytical toolkit still in use?
Because predictions and concepts can be judged separately. "The middle strata will disappear" is a proposition data can kill, and it died; "class is a relation rather than a ranking" is a measuring stick — it can be replaced, not falsified. Today's empirical schemas no longer split on ownership, yet they kept that way of asking: look first at what your relation to employment is, then at income. What to guard against is the move in reverse — using "the tool still works" to keep a failed prediction alive.
Can alienation be eliminated, or only reduced?
Abolishing the employment relation does not settle it by itself: someone working for themselves is still pushed along by orders, platform rules and repayment dates, with at least two of the four relations still cut. Conversely, Blauner's survey suggests that even with ownership unchanged, simply handing discretion back lowers powerlessness. The safer statement is that the four relations each have their own range of movement, and bundling them into a single all-or-nothing quantity makes the discussion impossible.
How do you say an idea "serves someone's interests" without it becoming an explanation for everything?
That is this tool's biggest risk: it is too usable, any claim can be explained this way, and so nothing is explained. To give it content you need evidence independent of the conclusion — through which channels is this account repeatedly produced? Are there equally popular accounts that cut against the same group's interests? Most important is the asymmetry: pointing to where a claim came from is not proof that it is false, and the reverse holds too.
