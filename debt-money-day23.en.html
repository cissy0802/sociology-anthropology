DAY 23 · THE ANTHROPOLOGY OF DEBT AND MONEY

The Anthropology of Debt and Money

The most counterintuitive line here: a society living by barter has never been found — credit came first, and the earliest money was for settling how much you owed in compensation

2026-09-16 · BigCat
He pays this time, you pay next time, and nobody has ever worked out the difference. Let someone hand you the change on the spot and the meal turns into something else.

You ask a neighbour to take in a parcel for you. Try to press twenty on him and he will probably be annoyed — not because it is too little, but because paying closes the matter, while what he was holding was a small unfinished obligation of yours.

Economics textbooks open with a different story: long ago people bartered, it was clumsy, so money was invented. Anthropologists have looked for over a hundred years and have not found that society. This page takes apart three things: where that story goes wrong, why still owing holds a relationship together better than settling up, and what the earliest money was actually for.

// 01

Anthropologists have looked for over a century and found no society that lived by barter

The textbook story runs roughly like this: the baker wants meat, the butcher wants shoes, the two do not match up, so everyone settles on some commonly wanted thing as an intermediary. Adam Smith wrote a version of it in 1776 and it has been retold for more than two centuries since — yet that society has never been found. The Cambridge anthropologist Caroline Humphrey put it bluntly in 1985: no example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described, let alone the emergence of money from it. Her paper was titled Barter and Economic Disintegration, and her observation ran the other way: large-scale barter typically appears after a monetary system has collapsed. It is a product of breakdown, not a starting point.

The mechanism is not hard to see. Barter requires a double coincidence of wants: I have what you want, you happen to have what I want, and it has to be at the same moment. But people who know each other do not need that coincidence at all — take the meat, we will remember. Credit does not need a double coincidence, only that the two of you will meet again.

The archaeology gives the same order. Mesopotamian temples and palaces were keeping accounts on clay from around 3000 BCE: the shekel, a weight of silver, served as the unit of reckoning, grain and silver had conventional conversion rates, and loans and interest were routine. The earliest struck coins appear only in late seventh-century BCE Lydia, in what is now western Turkey, made of a gold-silver alloy. Written debt precedes minted coin by more than two thousand years, and many of those tablets involved no metal passing hands at all, only a record of who owed whom how much.

The top row is the order that keeps getting retold; the bottom row is the order the tablets and the surviving coins give. The two ends have swapped places.

Evidence strength, stated plainly: Humphrey's claim is a negative one, and not found is not the same as does not exist. David Graeber's Debt: The First 5000 Years (2011) pushed the argument very broadly, and specialists have faulted a good many of its particular historical claims, so the two should be read apart; but the main line — that the barter myth does not hold and credit precedes coinage — is not much contested.

// 02

While it is still owed the relationship is still there; pay it off and the relationship ends

Back to the parcel. You offer the neighbour twenty and he is annoyed. He was holding a small obligation of yours, and that implied there would be a next time; pay, and the thread is cut. This is the most durable line in Graeber's book: a debt is an exchange not yet completed, and it is precisely the incompleteness that keeps the relationship running. The piece on gifts and reciprocity covered why giving outright binds harder than trading does; this is the same mechanism from the other end — anything that can be settled in one stroke is, once settled, no longer owed, and there is no longer any need to meet.

There is a weighty field case. In Evans-Pritchard's 1940 ethnography of the Nuer of South Sudan, a killing is settled in cattle — a substantial number, typically around forty head — mediated by a leopard-skin chief who belongs to neither side. The point is not what a life converts to in cattle. Those cattle were commonly used afterwards as bridewealth, so two families who would otherwise have gone on killing each other became affines instead. What compensation does is not conclude anything; it converts a relation that would keep killing into a relation that will keep circulating.

So when does a debt turn into something else? This is Graeber's decisive step: for an obligation to become a debt, three things have to be in place — a quantified amount, a determinate creditor, and a moment at which it can be discharged. With all three, it can be transferred, sold, and handed to a third party for enforcement. The favour you owe your neighbour cannot be assigned to someone else to collect; it is bound up with its being him. A sum written down can be, and whoever holds the note is the creditor. Quantification does not merely record the obligation clearly, it detaches it from this particular relationship.

The criticism this most deserves is that it slides easily into treating measurement itself as corruption. The other side is just as strong: a vague ledger of favours is exactly what can be drawn on without limit, because no figure is there to say enough; a written amount is, for the one who owes it, a protection, because it supplies an endpoint. And historical debt cancellations — Mesopotamian clean-slate edicts, the Jubilee in the Jewish tradition — work only because amounts are countable. Each reading is half right, and no side is taken here. Evidence strength: ethnographic cases plus historical textual interpretation, with no available experiment.

// 03

The earliest money was not for buying things, it was for working out how much to pay in compensation

What is a finger worth? The question sounds offensive, yet nearly every early law code answered it in earnest. The Law of Æthelberht of Kent, from around 600 CE and the earliest written law surviving in English, tariffs the items one by one: so much for a knocked-out tooth, so much for a severed finger, so much for striking an ear deaf. Germanic law even had a word for the whole idea, wergild, which means literally man-price.

The numismatist Philip Grierson argued in 1977 that compensation tariffs of this kind are a likely early source of the very idea of a scale of value. Their business is not trade but stopping a feud — for two families to negotiate at all, there has to be a schedule both accept. Anthropologists call such means of payment social currencies: used for marriage, compensation and fines, but never for buying tonight's dinner.

A fuller field case comes from central Nigeria. Working among the Tiv in the 1950s, Paul Bohannan described a set of ranked spheres of exchange: at the bottom, subsistence — yams, grain, small livestock, tools; in the middle, prestige goods — brass rods, cattle, white cloth, and historically people held as property; at the top, rights in marriage. Exchange within a sphere was everyday, converting upward was an achievement and converting downward a disgrace. Between spheres there was no rate at all — and precisely because there was none, nobody could sell enough grain to buy the right to marry.

What falls with the walls is not efficiency but possibility: what could not be done before is now merely expensive.

When general-purpose money arrived under colonial rule, the walls came down. Bohannan's 1959 paper is about the consequences: everything landed on one scale, grain converted to money, money to brass rods, brass rods toward the marriage sphere. The young gained a route upward, and elders whose standing rested on controlling the upper goods were hollowed out. The decisive property of general-purpose money is that it is general, not that it is convenient — it places classes of things that were incommensurable onto a single axis, and that step is social rather than technical.

The criticism belongs here too: the three-sphere model has since been faulted as too tidy, with later work holding that Tiv conversions were in practice more flexible and that monetisation partly predated colonial rule. It is powerful as a schema and should be discounted as history. The same goes for reading it forward: you do not transfer money to a friend who helped you move, nor put a price on a child's grades — those walls are still standing; converting attention, time spent queuing, or a recommendation into points is among the ones just taken down. Which walls ought to stand is not answered on this page. It states only something observable: remove a wall and flows that were blocked really do happen, and whoever was standing on that wall really does lose their footing.

🎒 In practice · BigCat

  • ① Engineering and system design—at every quarterly planning meeting, technical debt comes up, everyone nods, it goes into next quarter, year after year. This runs the trio from "while it is still owed the relationship is still there; pay it off and the relationship ends": amount, creditor, moment of discharge. Technical debt borrowed the urgency of the word debt without borrowing any of the three — nobody knows how much is owed, to whom, or when it is due, which is why it can always get into the queue and never to the front of it. What to change: for each item write down who is paying the interest (which team spends how many extra hours a week on it) and a review date; and stop using "technical debt" as the reason, saying instead "this eats four hours a week and has for three quarters".

🌀 Crossing over · Connections beyond the field

  • Complexity science · Once obligations are transferable, the path trouble travels has nothing to do with who knows whom: the model Gai and Kapadia published in Proceedings of the Royal Society A in 2010 gives a shape that keeps recurring — a network of institutions holding claims on one another is robust yet fragile: the more links there are, the more easily a single shock is diluted, but once a threshold is crossed the extent of the collapse is wildly large. This runs the trio from "while it is still owed the relationship is still there; pay it off and the relationship ends": once amount, creditor and moment of discharge are all in place, the obligation is detached from the particular relationship, can be transferred, and can be stacked link upon link. Which yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: to predict where trouble will travel, follow the links of amounts owed rather than the links of acquaintance — and the two graphs can fail to match completely, since the people you know do not owe you money and the people who owe you money you have never met. That is checkable: draw both the claims graph and the contact graph for the same set of organisations and compare which pairs are closest on each. But it does not hold here: models of this kind assume the amount on every link is given and additive, so the cascade is instantaneous. Debts of favour are neither additive nor transferable, there is no clearing house and no moment called default — a network of relationships does not fail as an instantaneous cascade but as slow avoidance and drift, and predicting it with contagion thresholds gets it flatly wrong.
  • Buddhist thought · A moral notation written very much like a ledger eventually grew a transfer function: Buddhism speaks of karma in highly quantified language — so many aeons, so many times over, so many portions. More interesting is what grew out of it later: the dedication of merit, assigning merit one has accumulated to someone else, usually a deceased relative. In form that is an assignment of a claim: a balance belonging to one party is moved into another's name. This runs the mechanism from "the earliest money was not for buying things, it was for working out how much to pay in compensation": once a commensurable notation is established, things that were not transferable acquire an interface for moving them, and nobody has to design that interface on purpose. Which yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: whether a tradition grows institutions of vicarious payment, transfer or offset depends not on how severe its doctrine is but on whether it uses a countable notation — a tradition that renders good and evil in stories cannot produce an assignable balance, and one that renders them in numbers eventually will. That is checkable: take the core texts of several traditions, count whether moral consequences are stated in numbers or in narrative, and see which ones developed vicarious-payment institutions. But it does not hold here: this ledger has no creditor. Nobody is collecting, there is no counterparty to negotiate with, and there is no court. The more forceful half of debt — third-party enforcement — is simply absent, so it cannot produce debt's most characteristic consequences: no collateral, no seizure, no bondage against the sum. Missing the enforcement half, what remains runs only on one's own belief in it.

Going deeper

If barter is not the origin of money, why has the textbook kept the story for two hundred years?
Because it was never history, it is a thought experiment: it shows what money solves, not where money came from. Told as history for long enough, it turns into an assumption about human beings — that a person is a trader first and social relations are added afterwards. The reversed order gives an entirely different assumption: people are first inside a tangle of owing and repaying, and exchanges that can be settled are cut out of that later.
Why do occasions involving money come wrapped in so much etiquette?
The mechanism from the piece on purity and danger connects here: the residue a classification leaves has to be handled, and money's whole talent is passing through every classification. Cash goes in red paper, bills get settled out of sight, money for an elder is not handed over directly — each of these re-marks an utterly undifferentiated thing as belonging to a particular kind of relationship. The more the relationship itself matters, the more wrapping the money gets.
Why is an amount that can be counted exactly so much easier to demand without embarrassment?
Because something countable can be taken outside the relationship and argued there. A favour has no stated size, so only the parties can judge it; a debt written as a number can be judged by any third party, and therefore handed to a third party to enforce. The moral language shifts with it: a vague obligation is spoken of as something remembered, a quantified one as something owed. Notice which obligations around you are sliding from the first language into the second.
