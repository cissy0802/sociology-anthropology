DAY 31 · SOCIAL MOVEMENTS

Social Movements

The counter-intuitive line of the day: how big a movement gets has almost nothing to do with how many people are unhappy.

2026-09-24 · BigCat
In 1965 an economist left sociology an awkward present. He argued that a group sharing a common interest is exactly why its members will not act together — and that the more of them there are, the less they act.

Third day with the lift broken. Twenty-odd people in the building's group chat are complaining, and complaining in unison. Nobody has phoned the management company.

You have probably seen the variants. Everyone privately says a process is broken, and when the meeting comes round nobody raises it. Everyone knows nobody reads that weekly report, and it still goes out.

The old explanation was that they were not angry enough — let the grievance build and people will move. The main finding of this field over the past sixty years runs the other way: grievance is almost never in short supply. What is missing is three other things, and any one of them can stall the whole thing on its own.

// 01

"Everybody wants it" is exactly why nobody does it

In 1965 Mancur Olson wrote a thin book called The Logic of Collective Action. Once the lift is fixed, the person who made the call and the twenty who did not are riding the same lift. A thing you cannot stop other people from using is a public good. Since you cannot stop them, the smart move for each person is to wait for someone else to call — free riding. The awkward part is that nobody here is being selfish: each person's arithmetic is correct, and the sum of it is that nobody moves. "We share an interest" does not get you to "we will act together". There is a gate in between.

Two things follow for Olson. First, small groups act more easily than large ones: three flatmates each get a third of the benefit, and if one of them does nothing the other two know at once; in a block of twenty flats the benefit is a twentieth each and nobody knows the neighbours' names. Second, a large organization gets moving on the back of things handed out only to participants — he called these selective incentives: a union's hardship fund and legal service, a trade association's market data, a congregation's help with weddings and funerals. His sharpest line is that many members of large organizations are in it for those, and the public cause is thrown in.

Be clear about how strong the evidence is: this is a theoretical argument plus historical examples, not an experimental result. The laboratory has an analogue, the public goods game — several people each get a sum of money, and whatever they put into a common pot is multiplied and split evenly. In the first round people typically put in about half; over more rounds contributions slide steadily down. Allow people to spend their own money punishing low contributors and cooperation holds up, a result that has been replicated many times. But in 2008 Benedikt Herrmann, Christian Thöni and Simon Gächter compared sixteen comparable participant pools around the world in Science and found places where people punish the high contributors instead, strongly enough to wipe out the benefit of punishment altogether. So "let people police each other" is not a switch you can throw anywhere.

// 02

Grievance is never what is short; money, people and a room to meet in are

In 1977 John McCarthy and Mayer Zald published a paper, since cited to death, that turned the question over in its opening lines: grievance is the constant, movements are the variable. Every era and every group carries a stock of complaints, and explaining the rise and fall of movements with something that does not vary will not get you far. The question is not why they are angry. It is why they managed to organize this time.

The Montgomery bus boycott makes the point, because the version everyone knows removes exactly this layer: one person was tired and would not give up a seat. Aldon Morris's The Origins of the Civil Rights Movement (1984) gives the other side. Rosa Parks was arrested on 1 December 1955; that night Jo Ann Robinson wrote a leaflet calling for a boycott at Alabama State College and, with three other people, ran the mimeograph machine all night — the usual figure is thirty-five thousand copies, and some sources give more — and the next day they went out across the city. Robinson belonged to the Women's Political Council, an organization of Black professional women that had been working on treatment on the buses for years and had lists, contacts and a printing machine.

The three hundred-odd days that followed also ran on things already in place: churches were the venues, church rolls were the address book, Black taxi drivers and private cars were assembled into a lift network. Doug McAdam's 1982 book takes the scale out to decades: Black churches, Black colleges and local branches of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People had been growing for a long time before the protest broke out, something he called indigenous organizational strength. The view has a cost: written carelessly, people turn into containers for resources. Later work brought grievance back, but in a different position — grievance shapes what a movement looks like, organization decides whether it gets off the ground. On evidence: this strand is mainly historical comparison, and its weak point is picking cases after the fact.

Three gates in series. With any one of them shut, no amount of stored grievance gets through — which is why "they were not angry enough" is usually the least important explanation.
// 03

Protest comes in a limited number of shapes, and they turn over by the generation

Charles Tilly and his assistants did a laborious thing: they coded, one by one, the "contentious gatherings" reported in British periodicals between 1758 and 1834, more than eight thousand of them. Going through the cards you can watch a gear change.

In the mid-eighteenth century the ready-made ways of expressing a grievance were these: stopping a grain wagon and selling its load at what the crowd held to be a fair price; pulling down enclosure fences; carrying someone who had offended the neighbourhood through the streets to be shamed; breaking one particular household's windows. All local, aimed at a specific person, and often needing someone of standing to pass the claim upward. By the 1830s a different set was in use: public meetings, signature petitions, marches in column, founding a dedicated association, starting a periodical — national, aimed at Parliament, speaking for itself.

Tilly called this limited set the repertoire of contention. The word repertoire is deliberate: a company only knows a few plays, and people do not protest in whatever way they please. Why only a few? Because the form has to be legible to both sides. Participants need to know where to stand, what to shout and when to disperse; the other side needs to know whether this is a bid to talk or a fight. Inventing a new form is expensive, and the expense is that nobody knows how to play along — but once a new form has been performed successfully a single time it spreads astonishingly fast. Sit-ins, occupations and human chains all went that way.

Tilly also left a checklist: a movement has to display four things outward — worthiness, unity, numbers, commitment. Why a march forms up neatly, why everyone fights over the crowd count, why people being beaten at a sit-in do not hit back, are each one of those four. On evidence: this is description resting on coded historical material, not causal identification.

// 04

The same event can be told as an injustice or as bad luck

The word frame is borrowed from Goffman — the man from the piece on dramaturgy — and means the account people use to see a pile of loose events as "what is going on here". The same event told as "the system filtered these people out" and told as "he was unlucky" mobilizes entirely different things. David Snow and colleagues wrote a 1986 paper on frame alignment, later tidied into three jobs that have to be done together: diagnosis (what the problem is, whose account it goes on), solution (what should be done) and the call (why you, why now). Leave one out and it jams. Diagnosis alone leaves people angry with nowhere to push; diagnosis and solution without a call takes you straight back to the gate in "'Everybody wants it' is exactly why nobody does it".

William Gamson ran a set of focus groups in 1992, letting ordinary people talk about nuclear power, affirmative action and issues of that kind. His observation: saying "the system has a problem" is not enough to mobilize, because you need an agent that can be named — an injustice frame needs a "who". But a frame has to be carried by people. Snow and colleagues studied recruitment to a Buddhist group in the United States in 1980 and found that approaching strangers in the street worked miserably; the overwhelming majority of new members came in along ties of family, friends and acquaintances that were already there.

The finest material comes from Mississippi Freedom Summer in 1964. More than a thousand students applied to do voter registration, and McAdam later found 1,068 application forms: 720 went, 239 withdrew at the last moment. Both groups had filled in the same form, and on attitudes you can barely tell them apart — the ones who withdrew did not care less. The difference is elsewhere: whether they had strong ties to someone already taking part, and whether they had prior experience inside an organization. He had also expected that people without full-time work or family commitments would be more likely to go (this is called biographical availability); it did not come out cleanly in this pool, and his own explanation is that the applicants had already been filtered once. This is a rare piece of material: the records were made in advance.

🎒 In the field · BigCat

  • ① Leading a team and organizations: you ask "does anyone have thoughts?" at the all-hands and the room is silent; once it breaks up, five people come to you separately in the corridor with the same problem. This runs the mechanism from "'Everybody wants it' is exactly why nobody does it" — the first person to speak carries the whole cost and the benefit goes to everyone. What to change: stop collecting in the open, gather it one-to-one beforehand, put the problem into the room yourself as the first speaker, and name one person to respond rather than leaving it to "everyone".
  • ② Engineering and systems design: every cross-team change burns two weeks on which route to take — write a document? start a channel? just open a change request? This runs the mechanism from "Protest comes in a limited number of shapes": a form is useful because both sides recognize it, and recognizing it is what saves you renegotiating how to play along each time. What to change: fix the routes for this kind of thing at two or three, post them, and stop picking the "most suitable" one each time — the payoff comes from the form being fixed, not from it fitting.

🌀 Crossing over · Other disciplines

  • Buddhist studies · If one monk inside the boundary is neither present nor has sent word, the whole meeting is void: a formal act of the monastic community in the Theravāda tradition is called saṅghakamma, and it starts by drawing a geographical boundary, the sīmā. Every monk inside it must either attend or send his consent (chanda) with a monk who does; if one is neither present nor has sent word, the act does not stand. This runs the mechanism from "'Everybody wants it' is exactly why nobody does it": free riding pays because absence costs nothing while the result still accrues to you. The procedure blocks that — silence is not consent, and staying away is not saving yourself trouble, it voids the whole thing. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise see: a group's capacity to act can be read off how it books absence, without measuring how keen anyone is. Any procedure that treats "no objection" as agreement is subsidizing free riding: not turning up costs nothing and is automatically counted into the majority. Changing the bookkeeping is cheaper than working up enthusiasm — move the default from "silence means yes" to "everyone states a position", and the cost shifts from the people in the room to the people who stayed away. But it does not hold here: the procedure rests entirely on that boundary, inside which the members are nameable and closed. A social movement has no boundary, and who counts as a potential participant is itself unclear. So this bookkeeping only means something when the roll is closed; where the roll is open, silence is not an action that can be recorded at all.
  • Evolutionary biology · Before two stags actually lock antlers, they work through a roaring contest and a parallel walk, in that order: on the Isle of Rum in Scotland, red deer contests in the rut go in fixed steps. First the roaring contest, the two stags roaring in turn at a quickening tempo until one of them suddenly stops and walks off; if that settles nothing they move to the parallel walk, pacing alongside each other and sizing one another up; only if that fails do they lower their heads and fight. Clutton-Brock and Albon's 1979 paper is about this sequence. Real fights are the minority, and the cost is concrete — as many as six per cent of rutting stags are permanently injured each year. This runs the mechanism from "Protest comes in a limited number of shapes": a form is useful because both sides recognize it, and recognition is what lets the information get through without paying the full price. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise see: the repertoire is small not because participants lack imagination but because of the constraint that the other side has to be able to read it. Run that backwards and it reads something else too: when one side starts using new forms the other cannot read, that is usually not a burst of creativity but a sign that the old reading has failed — the two sides no longer agree on what reaching this step means, so every round has to be played out to the end. But it does not hold here: the stags' steps were selected for and an individual has them without learning; a repertoire is learned, can turn over wholesale within decades, and gets rewritten by what the other side does — how the police handle it and how the courts rule both feed into the next round's forms. So "display saves you the fight" carries over and "the form is fixed" does not: a repertoire is a contract both sides are constantly revising.

Going deeper

If grievance does not explain movements, how should we look at the places where nothing happened?
The hardest thing to study is what did not happen — nobody files it. The organizational line at least gives you something checkable: are there existing organizations that cross groups, a venue you can borrow, a roll of names. What you cannot see is usually not the depth of people's patience, it is whether those few things were there.
Can selective incentives explain every kind of participation?
No. Going to Mississippi to register voters gets you no private benefit and may get you beaten. The later patch is to count "who you are going with" as an incentive too: letting a friend down has a cost. But once relationships also count as incentives, the framework holds anything, and therefore explains nothing.
Will online forms rewrite the repertoire?
Online forms cost almost nothing, so what they can prove is correspondingly small: a million signatures no longer means a million people willing to pay anything for it. The point of a repertoire is to make the cost visible, and once the cost is low the form stops moving anyone — which probably explains why the old forms that take time and put bodies in a place have come back.
