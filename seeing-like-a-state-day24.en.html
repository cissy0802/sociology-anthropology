DAY 24 · SEEING LIKE A STATE

Seeing Like a State

The counter-intuitive line of the day: to make the ledger accurate, the cheapest move has never been better measurement — it is remaking reality into the shape of the ledger. Forests, villages and surnames have all been remade this way.

2026-09-17 · BigCat
In a good many places your surname was not handed down by your ancestors. It was handed out one year by an official reading from a catalogue.

Every form you have ever filled in opens with the same boxes: name, address, identity number. It all looks self-evident. Yet fixed, inherited surnames are only a century or two old in much of the world, and the reason they appeared was stated plainly at the time — to collect taxes and to conscript soldiers.

This page takes apart three things: what governments have actually done to make their territory visible; what the knowledge that will not fit into a form looks like; and whether anyone has simply arranged their life so as not to be worth registering.

// 01

To count the trees properly, they replanted an entire forest — and a century later the second crop would not grow

In late-eighteenth-century Prussia and Saxony, foresters acquired a new instrument: the Normalbaum, a standard tree. Measure the timber volume of one standard specimen and you could extrapolate what the whole stand was worth. It was an elegant invention — for the first time a forest could be written into a budget.

The next step is the one that mattered. To make the ledger accurate, improving measurement is laborious; remaking the forest is cheap. So the undergrowth was cleared, the mixed trees felled, the leaf litter raked away, and in their place went single-species, same-age plantations in straight rows, mostly spruce and pine. Walking into one is like walking down a supermarket aisle. The first rotation grew superbly, yields were predictable, and officials were pleased.

The second rotation failed. About a century later the same ground was yielding twenty to thirty percent less, and German forestry science coined a word for the worst cases: Waldsterben, forest death. The reconstruction runs like this: the first crop had been living off soil capital the old forest had banked over centuries — humus, mycorrhizal fungi, insects, birds, fallen wood. None of it appeared in the ledger, because none of it yields timber. The ledger had not forgotten these things. It had no column that could hold them.

Evidence strength should be stated: this is a historical case with a retrospective reconstruction, not a controlled experiment. And the word Waldsterben in the 1980s was used mainly for forest damage attributed to acid deposition, which is a different matter from the monoculture story and should not be run together with it.

Usually only the upper arrow gets drawn. The trouble comes from the lower one — legibility does not merely observe, it turns round and alters what it observes.

James C. Scott's 1998 book distils this into a single mechanism he calls legibility. The point is not that states want to know more; everyone wants to know more. The point is that usable state knowledge has to be countable, comparable and verifiable from an office two hundred kilometres away. A fact that holds only for people who live there does not exist for that office.

Surnames came from exactly this. In 1849 the Spanish colonial administration in the Philippines issued the Clavería decree, accompanied by a catalogue of more than sixty thousand surnames, distributed province by province, town by town, alphabetically, one per household. The decree stated its reasons flatly: to register the population, to tax it, and to conscript from it.

The whole thing has to be said, though. The same apparatus is the precondition for public health, mass schooling, vaccination records and disaster relief — nobody can vaccinate a person they cannot find. Legibility is not a bad thing. It is a costly thing. The cost is in the next section.

// 02

Following every rule to the letter is the fastest way for workers to shut a factory down

There is a kind of strike in which nobody stops working. People come in as usual and simply carry out every regulation to the letter — the French call it grève du zèle, a strike of zeal — and production collapses. Nothing makes the point more directly: written procedure has never been self-sufficient. It has always been running on top of a large body of judgement that was never written down.

Scott gives that body of judgement a name: mētis, borrowed from ancient Greek and taken from Detienne and Vernant's 1974 study of Greek cunning intelligence. Its marks are easy to spot — local, situational, learnable only by doing, and half-destroyed the moment it is turned into a manual. The classic example is river pilotage. A large ship takes a local pilot aboard on the way into harbour, not because the visiting captain is a worse sailor, but because the sandbars in that channel move every year, and where they are this year is on no chart.

Agriculture supplies a solid piece of fieldwork. Paul Richards's Indigenous Agricultural Revolution (1985) describes intercropping among West African farmers, mostly in Sierra Leone: several crops in one plot, ripening at different times, rooting at different depths, the tall ones shading the short ones. Colonial agronomists, and their successors, long read this as backward and pushed single crops sown in rows. The reasons were not purely prejudice — a monocrop can be yield-measured, compared across sites, and supervised by an inspector, and a mixed plot allows none of the three. Richards argues that in local soils and rainfall, intercropping is frequently the more robust system: against drought, against disease, and in spreading labour across the season. Evidence strength: field research plus agronomic comparison, not a universal result — under other conditions monocropping does yield more.

The largest case of all is Tanzania's villagisation between 1973 and 1976. Dispersed households were moved into planned villages; the counts differ a great deal, with estimates running from somewhat over five million people to above ten million. The aims were not bad ones, and were partly achieved: you can only build a school, pipe water and send out an agricultural adviser where people live close together. But the village sites were fixed from plans and road lines, not from what each household had accumulated over decades about which plot to plant in which year, which slope dries out first, and which hollow actually carries a crop through a wet season. Yields fell.

The book's most transferable passage is its four conditions, which produce catastrophe only when they arrive together: administrative simplification, a confidence that the thing is now understood, a power able to impose it, and a society unable to resist. Take away one and you usually get an ugly project rather than a disaster.

The criticism belongs here too. Some readers find Scott too pessimistic about state capacity; others find he romanticises mētis — local knowledge also gets things wrong, also clings to practices that do harm, and being hard to articulate is not the same as being right. No side is taken here: both readings have cases that stand up.

// 03

Some people were never lost to the state — they arranged their lives so as not to be worth collecting from

Start with two kinds of crop. Grain grows above ground, ripens all at once, and stores and travels well: a tax collector riding out can estimate a field's yield at a glance, and has to turn up during the few days of harvest. Tubers like yams and cassava grow underground, get dug when they are wanted, and keep badly once lifted — collecting them means digging plot by plot. The same farming labour, and yet one of them is almost purpose-built for taxation and the other is almost impossible to tax.

The name Zomia was proposed in 2002 by the historian Willem van Schendel, from a Tibeto-Burman word for highlander. It refers to the belt of mainland Southeast Asian uplands above three hundred metres — roughly 2.5 million square kilometres, spanning several countries, home to something like a hundred million people. Scott's The Art of Not Being Governed (2009) offers a provocative reading of it: the traits that lowland states read as backward — shifting cultivation rather than settlement, oral transmission rather than writing, genealogies revised at will, dispersed social structures with no centre — may not be stages not yet reached, but secondary and strategic. Each one lowers the odds of being taxed, conscripted, or seized for forced labour.

Scott calls it the friction of terrain: the working border of a premodern state looks more like a contour line than like the straight lines on a map.

The criticism is heavy and deserves to be reported as it stands. Historians point out that uplands and lowlands traded, paid tribute and intermarried over long stretches of time, that a number of upland groups built polities of their own, and that evasion is nowhere near the only available explanation. Reading hundreds of differently descended groups as one category of evaders is itself a simplification — precisely the kind that the forestry section criticises. Scott himself holds that the argument largely stops applying after 1945, once roads, communications and survey had flattened the friction of terrain. Evidence strength: an interpretive argument in historical anthropology, not a testable causal claim.

What travels is not admiration for highlanders; swapping backward for clever still leaves them without a history of their own. What travels is colder: once something is used for extraction or assessment, it starts deforming towards whatever is hard to measure. The piece on organisations and institutions covered why organisations come to resemble each other; this is the other half of it — the party being measured moves too, and often moves faster.

🎒 In Context · BigCat

  • ① Leading teams and organisations — three months after a progress board goes live, the cards are all filled in neatly, and the things people are actually wrestling with have migrated into direct messages and scratch documents. This runs the mechanism from "Some people were never lost to the state": whatever is used for assessment deforms towards being hard to measure. That is not slacking, it is moving the work somewhere the collection cost is higher. What to change: stop adding fields and stop chasing updates, and instead ask once a week, "what did you not put on the board this week, and why?" — then pull the board out of the assessment loop entirely, because as long as it also feeds a score it is guaranteed to misreport.
  • ② Practice and the inner life — you log sitting practice in an app, it records minutes and a streak, and on a few days you sit exactly five minutes so as not to break the streak. This runs the mechanism from "To count the trees properly, they replanted an entire forest": once a countable proxy becomes the thing being watched, reality starts bending towards it. What to change: turn off the streak counter and record one sentence per session instead — where the mind kept going today. That sentence cannot be totalled or compared, which is exactly why it cannot be gamed.

🌀 Crossings · Where this shows up elsewhere

  • Distributed systems and AI · designing yourself to be uncountable costs you bandwidth least and self-knowledge most: censorship-resistant anonymity networks carry a class of modules called pluggable transports whose whole job is to make traffic look like something else, and peer-to-peer networks deliberately keep no enumerable central directory. This runs the mechanism from "Some people were never lost to the state": not hiding further away, but reshaping yourself so that the cost of counting exceeds the take. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach: the hard price of such systems is internal observability — nothing outside can enumerate them, and neither can they enumerate themselves, so they cannot report trustworthy user counts and struggle with global consistency. "Harder to attack" and "harder to govern yourself" are two faces of one property, and you cannot take only one of them. But here it does not hold: Zomia's friction comes from terrain, which is free and permanently there. Friction in a network is entirely designed and has to be maintained, and an adversary can reprice it by adding compute or changing the rules. So "distance itself gives shelter" simply fails here — stop maintaining it and it is gone the same day, whereas a mountain range does not flatten overnight.
  • Medicine and clinical trials · push the measurable number to its limit and more people die: type 2 diabetes care spent decades aimed at glycated haemoglobin, because it is measurable, comparable and can be written into a guideline. The ACCORD trial pushed it to the limit, with the intensive arm targeting a level below 6 percent. The trial was stopped early: at a median follow-up of 3.7 years, all-cause mortality was higher in the intensive arm (about 1.41 percent versus 1.14 percent per year), published in 2008. This runs the mechanism from "To count the trees properly, they replanted an entire forest": what got selected was a surrogate that can be checked remotely, while the link between it and the thing actually wanted — living longer — was assumed rather than measured. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach: wherever a surrogate is in use, "the more fully the target is met, the better" fails systematically, and the thing worth spending money to measure is that unmeasured link between the indicator and the goal, not a still lower value of the indicator. But here it does not hold: the local knowledge erased in Scott's cases is not retrievable by the centre and comes with no chance to roll back, whereas clinical medicine has randomised trials — ACCORD is precisely the mechanism by which this system caught its own error, and the guidelines did change afterwards. With a rollback-capable testing apparatus in place, his pessimistic conclusion does not follow.

Going deeper

If legibility costs this much, why does no modern state decline to do it?
Because a state that declines does not survive: no taxes means no army and no schools, and no register means no disaster relief. Scott's argument is not "do not see clearly" but "having seen clearly, do not assume that what you see is all there is." There is an observable test: does a plan leave any room to roll back if it turns out to be wrong — a pilot, a reversible step, permission for some places not to comply? The fourth of the four conditions, a society unable to resist, amounts to removing that rollback switch.
Can mētis be written down?
Partly — but the part that gets written down is no longer mētis. It becomes procedure, and then needs a fresh layer of unwritten judgement to carry it out. So the thicker the manual, the less tacit judgement is reduced; it only moves. Try it on the last onboarding document you wrote: wherever the first person following it gets stuck is where the mētis has relocated to.
Which indicators are hardest to deform?
Roughly the ones where, even if somebody optimises them ferociously, what comes out is precisely what you wanted. Pick a number currently being tracked around you and ask: if this were pushed to its extreme, would you still want the result? If the answer is no, it is a surrogate, and the link between it and the real goal is what needs watching. If yes, pushing on it is safe.
