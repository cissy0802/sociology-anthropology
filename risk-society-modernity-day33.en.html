DAY 33 · RISK SOCIETY AND MODERNITY

Risk Society and Modernity

The counter-intuitive line of the day: most of the dangers you are meant to guard against are ones you cannot feel at all — so "is this thing safe" has quietly been swapped for "can I trust how this system conducts itself".

2026-09-26 · BigCat
In June 1986 the British government put a ban on the sheep of the Cumbrian fells, and the notice said three weeks. The last restrictions came off in 2012.

Turn on the tap and you cannot judge whether the water is safe to drink.

It is colourless and tasteless, you cannot taste whether there is lead in it, and you cannot test it. All you can do is look up a water quality report, which comes from an institution you have never seen and rests on a method you have no way of checking.

So something has quietly been swapped. What you wanted to know is whether the water is drinkable; what you can actually judge is whether the institution issuing the report is trustworthy. Once that swap happens there is no going back, and it happens with almost every danger you meet in a day: food, medicine, the structure of your building, the air in your child's school.

// 01

You cannot taste what is in the water, so all you can do is read a report

In the spring of 1986 the German sociologist Ulrich Beck was close to finishing a book. Chernobyl exploded at the end of April, he added a passage about it to the preface of the German edition, and the book — Risk Society — became the most talked-about work of German-language sociology of the decade.

His opening move is a contrast. Industrial society fights over how good things are shared out: wages, housing, places at school. A new kind of contest is growing up alongside it, he says, over how bad things are shared out: who lives downwind, whose well sits downstream of the chemical plant, who got the batch that was contaminated.

These bads are not the old calamities, and they have three features. They are manufactured — not earthquakes and typhoons but side effects of some technical decision, so in principle someone made that decision. They are below the threshold of the senses — you cannot see radiation or smell an additive, so even the fact of your own exposure has to be established with instruments and experts. And they are unbounded in space and time — they cross borders and generations, and you cannot move away from them.

Together those three yield one consequential line: in the face of risk like this, "have I been harmed" is not a fact you can know by yourself. It is an entitlement that has to be established with knowledge and paperwork.

That line has a very concrete shape. The anthropologist Adriana Petryna spent several years doing fieldwork in 1990s Ukraine and wrote Life Exposed (2002). After Chernobyl, newly independent Ukraine turned a large share of its budget into radiation-related benefits and disability designations. What she saw was ordinary people having to make themselves into half-experts — learning dose units, learning which diagnoses can be attached to "radiation-related" — in order to convert the damage in their bodies into a status somebody recognizes; she calls it biological citizenship. This is a single ethnographic case, strong in detail and weak as a basis for generalization.

Beck also has a line that has been quoted to death: "poverty is hierarchic, smog is democratic." He means that the new risks have a boomerang effect, so that those who produce them cannot finally stay clear of them. The line is heavily contested: decades of work on environmental inequality find fairly consistently that exposure is in fact steeply stratified, with landfills and chemical parks landing more often where poor people and minority communities live. One reading says he overstated the equalization, and that bads are still distributed along the old hierarchy; another says his claim was never about equal exposure but about the novelty of there being no absolutely safe position. And it should be said plainly: Risk Society is a theoretical framework, not an empirical finding.

// 02

The experts said the ban would last three weeks; it ran for twenty-six years

At the end of April 1986 the Chernobyl plume reached Britain and rained out over the Cumbrian fells in north-west England; caesium settled on the grass. On 20 June the ministry of agriculture banned the movement and slaughter of sheep in Cumbria and Wales, covering some five thousand farms and more than two million animals. The notice said three weeks. After three weeks the ban was extended, and then extended again and again. The last restrictions came off in 2012 — twenty-six years.

How could the estimate be that far out? The scientists' forecast rested on a model in which caesium falling on soil is bound tightly by clay minerals, never reaches the grass, and is locked away within weeks. In the alkaline clay soils of the lowlands that is what happens. The Cumbrian uplands are not that: they are acid peat, where the caesium stayed chemically mobile, was taken up again through the grass roots, and went back into the sheep. The model had the wrong soil.

The sociologist Brian Wynne ran long interviews there in the late 1980s and in 1992 published "Misunderstood misunderstanding". What he found was not that the farmers failed to understand science: they barely disagreed about the magnitude of the hazard from caesium, and they followed the advice. What they refused was two other things. To run an experiment the scientists wanted sheep confined to small fixed plots; the farmers said that is not remotely how a hill flock is grazed, and that penned sheep and hill sheep are not the same animal in the relevant sense — and that was treated as amateur talk. And the scientists issued confident timetables, revised them, issued them again, and never conceded that their model might be wrong.

Wynne's conclusion is unusually hard for this field: what lay people judge when they decide whether to trust science is not the content of the knowledge but the conduct of the institution supplying it — whether it concedes that it might be wrong, whether it grants that your situation holds things it does not know, and what your relation of dependence on it is. Cumbria had a further layer: over the hill sat Sellafield, the big local employer, with someone from many of those households on its payroll. This is a qualitative case study, hugely influential, and also criticized for painting lay rationality too optimistically.

The skeleton of the episode was named by Anthony Giddens in The Consequences of Modernity (1990). Modern societies, he says, lift relations out of their local settings by two kinds of mechanism, which he calls disembedding: one is symbolic tokens like money, where you accept a piece of paper without needing to know who paid; the other is expert systems, technical knowledge organized so that it vouches, on your behalf, for what you cannot check yourself. You fly without knowing aerodynamics. That is not a gap in your education; that is disembedding working as designed.

But disembedding is not free. What you trust is not a person but a system you cannot see — Giddens calls it a faceless commitment. The system shows a face only in a few places, which he calls access points: the counter, the consulting room, the briefing. Your estimate of the whole system comes almost entirely from conduct at those few points, which gives them wildly disproportionate weight. Those meeting rooms in Cumbria were access points, and what happened in them was a confident tone plus repeated revisions.

It is not laziness that makes you judge by the last link. After disembedding, that link is all that is left open to you.
// 03

Nobody did anything wrong, and the risk got produced anyway

In January 1986 the space shuttle Challenger broke apart some seventy seconds after launch. The popular account is that someone approved a launch they knew to be dangerous. The sociologist Diane Vaughan spent nine years working through the tens of thousands of pages the investigation left behind, added interviews of her own, and in 1996 published The Challenger Launch Decision with a more uncomfortable answer.

The part that failed was the O-ring seal between segments of the solid rocket booster, and erosion of it had shown up on earlier flights. Each time, engineers wrote the report, held the review, compared this instance of erosion against previous ones, judged it "within the existing experience base", and cleared the flight. Next time the erosion was a little worse — but the baseline it was compared against had already been moved by the time before, so it was within the base again. Vaughan calls this the normalization of deviance: a signal that counted as an anomaly is gradually absorbed into the normal through a sequence of compliant, conscientious, fully documented judgements.

What matters is her negative claim. These were not reckless people, nobody concealed anything, and every step has a reason in the minutes. The danger was not decided by anyone; it accumulated. Beck had a name for a structure like that: organized irresponsibility — institutions producing hazards while the rules of attribution and proof turn those hazards into something nobody can be held to account for. One caution: this is a single-case historical and archival study, and "normalization of deviance" has since been used very loosely, losing explanatory force as it widens.

So what can trust rest on? One line of evidence that holds up points somewhere that does not look much like an answer: not the content, the conduct. The standard worry runs, hedge and you lose trust, so do not give ranges. In 2020 van der Bles, Spiegelhalter and colleagues ran five experiments with roughly 5,800 participants in total, one of them embedded directly in the BBC News website. Giving a numerical range did make people see the number as more uncertain — as it should — but trust in the source fell only slightly; what cost relatively more was expressing the uncertainty in vague words. The effects are all small and the direction is stable.

Back to Cumbria. What the farmers complained about was neither the severity of the ban nor the toxicology of caesium. It was being treated as people who knew nothing, and a tone that would not say "we may have got this wrong". Trust is an estimate of an institution's conduct, and that estimate has an observable consequence: how many people follow the next announcement.

🎒 Scenes · BigCat

  • ① Parenting and education: Every year or two a claim surfaces that some thing is bad for children — a plastic, an additive, a screen-time number. You look into it, find people arguing both ways, and end up either avoiding it altogether or pretending you never saw it. The mechanism running here is the one from "you cannot taste what is in the water, so all you can do is read a report": hazards of this kind are below the threshold of the senses, so "how dangerous is it" can only be obtained by way of a knowledge system. What to change: drop the binary question "is this safe" and ask instead whether a magnitude of exposure is given — how much, for how long. A conclusion that states no dose triggers no action.
  • ② Leading a team and organizations: Before anything new ships it goes through a risk review, the template gets longer every year, and the number of incidents does not move. The mechanism is the one from "nobody did anything wrong, and the risk got produced anyway": deviations are absorbed into the normal one compliant step at a time, risk accumulates out of a pile of compliant records, and there is no attributable moment — so a longer review document produces a distribution of responsibility, not a reduction of risk. What to change: drop the field that asks "which risks did you consider" and replace it with "when this goes wrong, who finds out first, and how long does that take".

🌀 Crossing over · Notes from other fields

  • Physics and mathematics · An insurer can work out in advance which things it cannot insure, and the test is two mathematical properties: Insurance works by pooling a large number of mutually unrelated small losses so that the average settles down — which requires the losses to be independent and the tail of the distribution not to be too heavy. What is running here is the last two features of the new risks from "you cannot taste what is in the water, so all you can do is read a report": one event strikes every policyholder at once across space and time (correlated), and the loss has no upper bound (heavy-tailed). Once both hold, pooling no longer shrinks the relative variation, the premium has to approach the maximum possible loss, and nobody can buy it. From that follows a conclusion you would not otherwise see: "uninsurable" is neither rhetoric nor a political stance but a property you can compute in advance; any technology meeting those two conditions will see commercial insurance withdraw of its own accord, and continued operation then requires legislation to cap liability. That prediction has been tested — the American Price-Anderson Act of 1957 exists precisely because no insurance company would underwrite a nuclear plant, and it put a ceiling on liability for a nuclear accident. But this is where it stops holding: that mathematics requires the loss to be writable as a number. Property and medical bills can be; the part Beck emphasizes — exposure across generations, whether you can still live here — is not "hard to estimate", it is that no probability distribution is defined on that space at all. So what carries over is why private insurance withdraws by itself; what does not carry over is how large the total loss to society is. The latter is not a harder number. It is not a number.
  • Psychology · Given the same set of hazards, the ordering experts produce tracks annual fatalities and the ordering ordinary people produce does not: In 1987 Paul Slovic reviewed a method in Science: have people rate dozens of hazards, then factor-analyse the ratings, and two dimensions fall out — dread (uncontrollable, catastrophic, inequitably distributed) and the unknown (imperceptible, new, not understood by science). Lay orderings track those two dimensions. What is running here is the mechanism from "you cannot taste what is in the water, so all you can do is read a report": the hazard is below the senses, so the quantity "how dangerous" exists only by way of a knowledge system. From that follows a conclusion you would not otherwise see: the claim that the public cannot handle numbers is a misreading — the two sides are not at different precisions on one quantity, they are measuring two different quantities. So piling more numbers on the public should not narrow the disagreement, because numbers change the experts' quantity, while lay ratings contain "uncontrollable" and "not understood", and what would move those is institutional conduct: who can halt it, who answers when it goes wrong. But this is where it stops holding: what Slovic measured was perceived ordering of a given list on a questionnaire — not behaviour, and not trust in institutions. The farmers in "the experts said the ban would last three weeks; it ran for twenty-six years" are precisely not on those two axes: they did not dispute the magnitude of the hazard from caesium, they were judging the institution's conduct. So what carries over is that the two sides are not measuring the same quantity; what does not carry over is assigning lay judgement wholesale to perceptual dimensions.

Going deeper

If a risk has to be manufactured to count as new, what were the old plagues and famines?
They were just as uncontrollable and just as cross-generational. Beck's dividing line is not the scale of the consequence but the structure of attribution: a plague leaves no decision record you can pull, and a chemical plant does. The reverse holds too — once something is judged to have been decided by someone, it turns from fate into a dispute.
Is being imperceptible the nature of the new risks, or only a result of our measuring better?
The same dose was unknown to anyone a century ago, so it did not then constitute an object of risk. Which means the capacity to measure is itself producing objects of risk: every drop in the limit of detection can turn a set of problems that did not exist into problems that do.
Can trust in a system and trust in a person substitute for each other?
On Giddens's account a single face at an access point vouches for the whole system, so one experience at a counter can change your estimate of an enormous institution. What is suspect is whether the substitution is symmetrical: standardizing a process until the person does not matter may make service steadier while closing the only channel through which conduct can be observed.
