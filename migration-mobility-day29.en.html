DAY 29 · MIGRATION AND MOBILITY

Migration and Mobility

The counter-intuitive line of the day: when a place gets richer, the number of people leaving it usually goes up first, not down.

2026-09-22 · BigCat
People from one small district end up running the same kind of shop thousands of kilometres away. Not because that trade suits them — because the first person made the route work, and everyone after paid a little less for it.

You have probably seen a version of this: the small restaurants along one street, and when you ask, every owner comes from the same district. Or a team at work where half the CVs name the same previous employer. Where people go looks like each person doing their own arithmetic — but the answers cluster far too tightly for that.

This page covers three things: why people leave, and why "people leave the poorest places" does not fit the data; whether leaving means you have arrived; and what happens to the generation that grows up on both sides.

// 01

"People leave the poorest places" does not fit the data

The tidiest story is a push and a pull: conditions here force you out, wages there draw you in, and the bigger the gap the more people move. Someone wrote a version of this more than a century ago, and it is still the default explanation in the news.

The trouble is that the poorest countries are not the ones sending the most migrants. In 2014 Michael Clemens reviewed forty-five years of research and set it out plainly: as a country's income per head rises from a low base, its emigration rate typically rises first, and only starts to fall once the country reaches the upper-middle-income range. That rise-then-fall curve is called the migration hump.

The reason is not mysterious: leaving costs money — documents, fares, agents, and the months of living expenses before the first wage arrives. People who are very poor are not uninterested in leaving; they cannot afford it. A little more income means a few more people who can. So across a long stretch, development is not the cure for emigration but its fuel. This rests on cross-country panel correlations, and individual countries are heavily shaped by policy and war, so do not take it as an iron law.

The orange line is the hump that keeps turning up in cross-country data; the dashed blue line is the monotonic decline push-pull expects — at the poorest end the two run in opposite directions.

The demand side counts too. Michael Piore's Birds of Passage (1979) argued that wealthy economies grow a particular class of jobs — unstable, with no ladder, jobs locals feel demeaned by; but for someone planning to work a few years and go home, the meaning of that job is cashed out back there: a house built, a wedding held, younger siblings put through school. Labour does not simply flow to wherever there is a shortage.

What really explains the clustering is networks. The Mexican Migration Project began with four communities in 1982 and has run every year since 1987, going house to house collecting migration histories. From it Douglas Massey and colleagues drew out cumulative causation: each person who gets a foothold abroad lowers the cost for the next person from the same village — somewhere to sleep, someone to walk you to work, knowledge of which route works. Migration produces its own causes.

The boundary was found by the same researchers: using the same data on Mexican urban areas in 2004, Fussell and Massey found cumulative causation largely absent there, because urban networks are already dispersed. So push-pull is not wrong so much as wrongly scaled: it computes national average incomes, while what actually moves is specific people along specific relationships — and those relationships form a network in towns and villages, and do not in large cities.

// 02

They did not "leave" — they live in two places at once

Peggy Levitt did something unusual for her time: she lived at both ends. She did fieldwork in a Dominican village called Miraflores and also in Jamaica Plain in Boston, where people from that village had settled. The book, The Transnational Villagers, appeared in 2001.

One figure she recorded says a great deal: by 1994, more than sixty-five per cent of the village's 445 households had relatives in the Boston metropolitan area. Village decisions waited on what people in Boston thought; meanwhile how a child was raised in Boston, or who was seeing whom, remained subject to village opinion. This is not a village plus a migrant enclave — it is one village spread across two places.

What flows back is not only money. In 1998 Levitt proposed the idea of social remittances: practices and standards travel too — how a meeting should be run, who decides things at home, what counts as respectable. Money leaves a paper trail and these do not, but what they alter is the yardstick people judge by, and that is often harder to reverse than cash.

The money is not small either: the World Bank estimates that remittances to low- and middle-income countries reached roughly $685 billion in 2024, more than those countries received in foreign direct investment — and that counts only formal channels. As the piece on the anthropology of debt and money argued, where money goes is never only an economic question: remittances are at once income, obligation, and a position in the hierarchy back home.

The name for all this came from Nina Glick Schiller and colleagues in 1992: transnationalism, the argument that migrants do not move from one container into another but maintain a social field across both. A decade later she and Andreas Wimmer named a defect in the social sciences themselves — methodological nationalism, the default assumption that nation equals society equals the natural unit. Statistics are collected by border and surveys fielded by border, so anything spanning borders becomes invisible: not ignored, just absent from the table.

The counter-argument comes from inside the same camp. Surveying Colombian, Dominican and Salvadoran immigrants, Alejandro Portes and colleagues found that only a small share made cross-border business a regular livelihood; most put their weight on where they had landed. So: transnationalism is overstated as a general way of life, and sound as a category of phenomena the old framework missed entirely. Both sentences have to be said.

// 03

The children who "fit in" fastest do not necessarily end up doing best

For the first half of the twentieth century the expectation about immigrants' children was a straight line: each generation moves closer to the mainstream, with language, occupation and intermarriage following in order, and time solving the problem by itself. The idea came from the Chicago School's race relations cycle, systematised by Milton Gordon in 1964.

In 1993 Alejandro Portes and Min Zhou proposed something different, segmented assimilation: not one line but several forks. Which one you take depends on what kind of labour market you land in, whether there is still a functioning community around you, and which category other people sort you into.

They then ran a long-term study: from 1992 they surveyed more than five thousand eighth- and ninth-grade children of immigrants in schools in Miami and San Diego, following them into the mid-2000s. One result runs against intuition: children who kept their parents' language and stayed connected to an ethnic community did not on average do worse on academic and psychological measures, and on some did better. They call this selective acculturation.

The point of segmented assimilation is not which path is better, but that the forks exist — and which one you take is not decided by individual effort alone.

Why would keeping the mother tongue help? Not because language has any magic in it, but because of parental authority. Children learn fast and parents slowly, and the household undergoes a role reversal: the child reads the contracts, makes the phone calls, talks to the teacher. The person who can speak becomes the person who decides, and the limits and backstop parents could provide stop working — not because they no longer want to provide them, but because they can no longer deliver them. What selective acculturation preserves is a channel through which things can still be discussed.

The counter-argument is equally strong. Richard Alba and Victor Nee's Remaking the American Mainstream (2003) points out that the mainstream itself expands — a great deal of what now counts as mainstream was carried in by an earlier round of immigrants and absorbed; emphasising the downward path too heavily understates how much of the long-run data still shows most groups moving up. The argument continues.

On evidence strength: that study covers two metropolitan areas, a particular period and a particular ethnic composition, so do not transplant it elsewhere as a law. What survives is the mechanism — acculturation is not a single slider from zero to one; both repertoires can be held at once, and both can fail to take hold at once.

There is a further layer, made in the piece on race and ethnicity: boundaries run in two directions. How you identify yourself is one thing; which category other people sort you into is another, and that one is not yours to change. The feeling the second generation most often reports, of falling slightly short on both sides, is frequently not an identity that failed to form but two systems of classification running at once, and failing to line up.

🎒 Scenarios · BigCat

  • ① Leading teams and organizations—a year into growing the team you look back and more than half the new hires come from the same former employer or the same small circle, and the share is still rising each quarter. This is the cumulative causation mechanism from "'People leave the poorest places' does not fit the data": once the first person is in, the next from the same source costs less — someone vouches for them, and what they say in the interview lands immediately. The channel thickens itself, regardless of the channel's quality. What you can change: on the hiring dashboard, replace "share of referrals" with "concentration of referral sources" — how much the top three sources contribute. When that number climbs steadily, go build other channels instead of raising the referral bonus.
  • ② Investing and position sizing—the yearly stocktake of what you own, with the table organised by where the accounts are: these ones here, those ones there. This is the methodological nationalism mechanism from "They did not 'leave'": collect data along container boundaries and the exposure that crosses containers never enters the table. What you can change: rewrite the table on a different axis, sorted by which currency the money will actually be spent in over the next five years, then line that up against the currency the assets sit in. Stop labelling assets "domestic" or "foreign" by where the account is: that records the container, not the exposure.

🌀 Crossing over · Connections across disciplines

  • Complexity science · Mix conductive particles into plastic and below a certain proportion it does not conduct at all; add a little more and it suddenly does: an everyday occurrence when making antistatic materials. While the particles are sparse each is isolated and there is no continuous path for current; once they are dense enough to cross a critical value, the first path spanning the whole block appears and resistance falls off a cliff. The mathematics of this is percolation, and what it computes is when connectivity suddenly appears. It runs the cumulative causation mechanism from "'People leave the poorest places' does not fit the data": each additional person already established abroad is one more edge added to the network. That yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: to predict whether a place is about to start sending large numbers of people out, measuring how many usable ties remain between locals and those already outside tells you more than measuring the wage gap; and the same push on either side of the threshold produces completely different flows — "nothing has moved in all these years" and "why can't it be stopped now" can be two sides of one parameter. But this does not hold here: in percolation every path is equivalent, passive and indestructible. In a migrant network an edge is a specific person under obligation, who can host only so many, who chooses whom, and whose resources run out. So it does not grow an ever-larger connected cluster above the critical point but tops out — which is exactly what Fussell and Massey's null result in urban samples means: a network whose edges saturate has no true percolation threshold.
  • Psychology · Show the same people an American symbol before they interpret a picture, or a Chinese symbol before the same picture, and their account changes: the experiment is Ying-yi Hong and colleagues, 2000. The participants were Chinese Americans raised in both cultures. They were shown a set of cultural primes and then asked to interpret an ambiguous scene: a shoal of fish with one swimming out in front. Those primed with American cues were more likely to say it wanted to swim ahead; those primed with Chinese cues were more likely to say the shoal behind was pushing it. This runs the selective acculturation mechanism from "The children who 'fit in' fastest": two frameworks can sit in one person at the same time, invoked by cues, rather than trading off against each other. That yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: what a one-dimensional "degree of integration" scale measures may not exist — a person can be near the top on both frameworks. Acculturation would be better recorded as which repertoires someone can invoke and what cues invoke them, not as a position between zero and one; which also explains why the same person's questionnaire answers in different settings contradict each other — not dishonesty, but a questionnaire that swapped the cues along with the setting. This kind of switching has been replicated across ethnic groups and tasks, but it belongs to the priming paradigm, a whole class that took a beating in psychology's replication crisis, so the robustness of any single effect has to be judged separately. But this does not hold here: frame switching is triggered by cues on your side, so you can work it; which category other people sort you into does not shift with whichever framework you invoke. A lab can change the prime for you; it cannot change the classification in the head of the person across from you. So "switching freely" holds at the level of how you think, and not at the level of how you are treated.

Going deeper

If migration networks thicken themselves, how did the first person go?
This is the thinnest part of the theory. Cumulative causation explains why it snowballs, not how it started. Historically the start often comes from outside: labour contracts, war, railway building, colonial administrative arrangements. So the direction a place sends people today can often be traced to some decision made decades ago that had nothing to do with what locals wanted.
How do you tell "living in two places" apart from "settled in neither"?
Look for one concrete thing: is there an issue that both sides must discuss together before it can be settled? Village business in Miraflores was exactly that. If each side simply gets on with its own life and all that remains is greetings at holidays, those are two parallel lives, not one transnational social field. What distinguishes them is not depth of feeling but whether decisions are shared.
Why does the word "integration" start an argument so easily?
Because it ties a descriptive question to a normative one. "How does a group's language use change across generations" is measurable; "what a person ought to become" is not a measurement question. A mechanism explains nothing about what should be done — and in an argument, each side is usually talking about only one of the two.
