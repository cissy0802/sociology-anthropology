DAY 20 · KINSHIP

Kinship

The most counterintuitive line here: this system allocates positions rather than blood — a woman can occupy the slot marked "father", and a dead man can occupy one indefinitely

2026-09-13 · BigCat
Father's sister, mother's sister, mother's brother, father's elder brother, father's younger brother: in Chinese that is five people and five separate words. English has two. Neither language is more precise than the other; they are doing different jobs.

Kinship looks like the least sociological subject there is. Parents, brothers, sisters — surely that is biology. Yet this discipline has spent more than a century on it, filling pages with diagrams of triangles and circles, for a solid reason: where there is no state, no company, no court and no piece of paper to sign, kinship is the operating system. Who raises whom, who farms which land, whose side you take in a fight, who carries on living in your place after you die — it assigns all of that.

The counterintuitive part is that it does not consult blood when it assigns. It lays out a set of positions first, hangs a set of obligations on each, and only then decides who steps into them. This page takes apart three things: how positions work, what the rule about whom you may not marry is really managing, and what kind of map those terms in your mouth actually are.

// 01

He went to South Sudan to count cattle and found he was counting kinship relations

In 1930 an Englishman travelled to the southern part of what was then Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, now South Sudan, to study a cattle-keeping people called the Nuer. His name was Evans-Pritchard, and the government there had commissioned the work; the reason it wanted the work done was not creditable, since the government had just finished fighting the Nuer. He put that on the record himself — one morning a government force surrounded his camp hunting two prophets who had led a recent revolt, and took hostages. The Nuer were unusually unwelcoming to outsiders in that period, he wrote, because their recent defeat and the measures that followed had left deep resentment. An observation can be accurate while the position it was gathered from stays on the table.

What he wanted to work out was what held these people together. The answer was in the cattle. A marriage is not complete until cattle have been handed over to the bride's kin, and what matters is not how many but which animal goes to which relative — how many head to the mother's brother, how many to which man on the father's side, all of it fixed. One distribution of cattle recites the family's kinship structure out loud. Fights work the same way: whose side you take is not a matter of who your friends are, but of which level of the genealogy the two households branch apart at.

What really overturns the idea that kinship is blood is two kinds of marriage. One is ghost marriage: a man dies without sons, and a brother or nephew marries a wife in his name. The living pay the cattle, but the children belong legally to the dead man, carry his name, inherit his herd and take over his ritual position. In Evans-Pritchard's records this kind of marriage was too common to treat as a special case. The other is woman-marriage: a woman who cannot bear children can pay cattle herself and take a wife, and she is then the father of those children, while the man who actually makes the wife pregnant holds no paternal position in the reckoning at all.

Two words need separating here, and both are used below: pater, the social father, the person occupying the position; and genitor, the begetter. Evans-Pritchard's record is blunt about it — the Nuer paid little regard to the manner of begetting so long as legal fatherhood was well established. Position first, person second. On evidence strength: this is single-site ethnography from the 1930s, resting on one person's observation with no third-party data to check it against. The Nuer live in South Sudan today; they are people, not specimens.

// 02

The rule about whom you may not marry is really a rule about whom you must give people to

Incest prohibitions are everywhere, and for a long time that universality was treated as the puzzle: why does everyone forbid it? Is it instinct? In The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) Lévi-Strauss changed the question. The reverse side of a prohibition is a command. Forbidding you to marry your own sister amounts to requiring you to give her to someone else — and now there is a relationship between your household and another that has to be maintained. The taboo is not managing prevention. It is enforcing exchange.

Exchange runs in two modes. Restricted exchange: two groups swap sisters, a closed little loop, stable, but it links only two households. Generalised exchange: A gives to B, B to C, C back to A — an open long circle that can chain many groups into one fabric, at the price of giving someone away first and waiting a turn to be repaid. It runs on credit, the same skeleton as the deferred return in the topic on gifts and reciprocity.

The most telling part is one detail. Your father's brother's daughter and your mother's brother's daughter are exactly the same genetic distance from you. Yet in many patrilineal societies the first counts as a sister and marrying her would be incest, while the second is precisely the preferred spouse. One biological distance, cut into two opposite categories. The basis is not mysterious: the first is inside your own descent group, the second is in the group you are meant to exchange with. The ruler doing the cutting is the exchange relation, not degree of relatedness.

Schematic. Triangles are men, circles women; horizontal lines are marriage, vertical lines descent. The two cousins stand at exactly the same genetic distance from "you" but fall into opposite categories — on the basis of which group each of them is in.

Three layers of criticism belong on the table. First, the scheme writes women as objects exchanged by men, and that is the heaviest cut. Second, there is a large-scale counterexample: across much of the Arab-Islamic world the preferred spouse is precisely the father's brother's daughter, the class the structural logic rules out; Korotayev's cross-cultural statistics match the distribution of that preference to the reach of eighth-century Arabisation and Islamisation. The structure is not a universal law. Third, and most important, Rodney Needham's clarification: rules of this kind are not statistical preferences of the "most people really did marry a cousin" sort, but classifications that sort everyone into marriageable and non-marriageable. The proportion of actual marriages made with the genealogically exact cousin is often low, for a deflating reason — in a small population that person may simply not exist. On evidence strength: this is a structural model built out of ethnographic reports and terminology tables, not measured marriage behaviour, and Francis Korn's case-by-case re-examination in 1973 concluded the classification does not survive the details.

// 03

The word you use for your father's sister is a map of who may marry whom

Back to the family table. Father's sister and mother's sister, father's elder brother and father's younger brother, paternal cousin and maternal cousin — in Chinese you keep all of them straight. English packs four people into aunt and eight kinds of relative into cousin. The first person to treat this as data was Lewis Henry Morgan, whose Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family was published by the Smithsonian in 1871. His method was new for the time: printed schedules mailed out through missionaries, Indian agents and consuls to be filled in around the world, yielding terminologies from some hundred and forty societies — anthropology's earliest global survey. His own interpretation, that terminologies are fossils of earlier marriage forms, was overturned; the data survived. In 1949 George Murdock sorted the tables into six types. English is the Eskimo type, distinguishing only lineal from collateral kin, with one word covering a father's brother and a mother's brother alike. Chinese is the Sudanese type, with a separate term for nearly every position, the most finely divided of the six.

Stated plainly, the mechanism is modest: what terminology does is sort people into equivalence classes — everyone under one term owes you the same set of obligations. Where terms merge and where they split is not arbitrary. In Iroquois-type systems father and father's brother share a term while mother's brother gets his own, and that line is exactly the line of whose daughter you may marry. Terminology table and marriage rule are two faces of one thing, which is also why the rule in "The rule about whom you may not marry" can actually run: you do not have to remember the genealogy, only what to call the person.

Further down comes a heavy piece of self-examination. In A Critique of the Study of Kinship (1984) David Schneider argued that the discipline had quietly assumed kinship was reproduction, taking one Euro-American folk theory — blood — as a universal skeleton. His American Kinship (1968) had already made the case that "blood relation" is itself a cultural doctrine rather than a natural fact. That is a conceptual critique rather than a measurement, and no consensus has settled inside the field.

What makes the contemporary echo interesting is that the most exotic-looking distinction of all is now used in court every day. Assisted reproduction has split the genitor position into several slots — egg donor, gestational surrogate, legal parents — and what family courts rule on is exactly the pater/genitor difference, only written into judgments. On another side, Kath Weston did fieldwork in the San Francisco Bay Area for Families We Choose (1991), recording how lesbians and gay men used the word family to do the same jobs: care, obligation, who counts as the inner circle. And the most everyday layer of all: calling an unrelated person uncle or auntie, or a fellow student in a teaching lineage elder brother, does the same work — putting someone into a class, and the class brings its obligations with it.

🌀 Crossing Over · Interdisciplinary Links

  • Evolutionary biology · Squirrels decide whether to give an alarm call by degree of relatedness, humans decide by terminology, and the two ledgers part company at one point: in 1964 Hamilton gave a formula — an altruistic act can be retained by natural selection when the benefit to the recipient times the relatedness between the two exceeds the cost to the giver. In 1977 Sherman spent three summers and some three thousand hours watching Belding's ground squirrels in the California mountains: males disperse on reaching adulthood while females stay where they were born, so the females in one patch are relatives, and those with close kin nearby give alarm calls more often when a ground predator approaches — and the caller is likelier to be the one taken. Later work tightened the boundary: calls to aerial predators look more like self-preservation. The mechanism from "The rule about whom you may not marry": two people at identical relatedness get cut into opposite categories. Which yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: when you predict human helping from relatedness coefficients, the direction of the error is foreseeable rather than random noise — what to consult is not the pedigree but the terminology table. But this does not hold here: Hamilton's formula tracks gene frequencies across generations, and the coefficient is actual — renaming people does not shift it. Wherever the outcome lands on genetic consequences, social classification does nothing: the two kinds of cousin carry no difference in inbreeding risk.
  • Physics and mathematics · Someone ran marriage rules through permutation-group theory and found some conceivable rules cannot run at all: the 1949 book carries a mathematical appendix by André Weil, then at the University of Chicago, applying permutation groups to several types of marriage law. The approach is direct: sort everyone into a finite number of marriage classes, and then "which class of man marries which class of woman" and "which class their children enter" are both maps from the set of classes to itself. Whether a rule can keep running long-term becomes the question of whether those maps compose into something closed, or send some people into a position with nobody they may marry. The mechanism from "The word you use for your father's sister": terminology sorts people into a finite set of equivalence classes and the rule acts on classes rather than individuals — which is precisely a map on a finite set. Which yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: whether a marriage rule is internally consistent can be checked without any fieldwork at all; among rules that sound equally reasonable, some are mathematically impossible to sustain, and it is no coincidence that the field never finds them. But this does not hold here: the algebra requires each person to belong to exactly one class and the rule to be followed strictly, and real systems satisfy neither. Guillot and colleagues ran simulations against genome-wide data from an Indonesian community in 2015 and hit exactly this: strict adherence depresses genetic diversity, while the actual data look more like loose compliance. The algebra is accurate about which rules are impossible and silent about what people actually do.

Going Deeper

Chinese separates father's sister from mother's sister and English does not. Which is "more precise"?
The question is pitched at the wrong level. Precision is relative to the job being done: if property and obligation run down two separate tracks, paternal and maternal, then not distinguishing them makes the job impossible; and if they do not run separately, the extra distinctions have nothing hanging on them and will loosen over time. The checkable version is to look at what consequences these distinctions still carry — who must attend a funeral, how an inheritance divides.
How many "fathers" can one person have? When law, genetics and raising a child come apart, which one counts?
The Nuer had already split it into two, and assisted reproduction has split it into more. Anthropology cannot supply the answer; what it supplies is a reminder that the three have never automatically coincided, and that "the natural family" is itself a cultural doctrine. Admitting it is a doctrine is the precondition for arguing about how any case should be decided.
Can "marriage" have a definition that holds everywhere?
Working on the Nayar of Kerala in 1959, Kathleen Gough ran into an arrangement with neither co-resident spouses nor exclusive conjugal rights, yet in which children's status was perfectly clear; the definition she arrived at had to go around the couple and start from the child. Definitions being forced toward abstraction is the normal condition of comparison at this scale — the more cases one covers, the less can be derived from it.
