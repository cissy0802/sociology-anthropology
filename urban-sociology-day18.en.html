DAY 18 · URBAN SOCIOLOGY

Urban Sociology

The most counterintuitive line here: almost nobody in a neighbourhood need be pushed out for the whole street to have changed hands

2026-09-11 · BigCat
Walk two hundred metres left out of a station and two hundred metres right: the signs, the passers-by, the price of a bowl of noodles are all different — same administrative district, three minutes' walk.

You have probably had this moment: in a city you have lived in for years you turn down a street you have never walked, and suddenly feel you should not be here. Nobody says anything, nothing happens; it is just that the density of shops, the speed people walk at, who is looking at whom, have all switched to another setting.

Cities sort people into groups by address, and those groups then live startlingly different lives. This page takes apart three things: how the earliest researchers saw this, and what they missed; how much force "where you live" actually has; and where the original residents go when a district gets expensive. The answer to the last one runs against intuition, and the disagreement turns out to be about measurement aperture, not about which side you are on.

// 01

Someone moved into a "chaotic" district for three and a half years and found the order there terrifyingly dense

In 1920s Chicago the population had multiplied several times over within decades, and new migrants were packed into the ring of old housing around the centre. The University of Chicago had a Robert Park who had spent more than a decade as a newspaper reporter before turning academic, and his demand on students was famous: don't sit in the library, get out on the street, get the seat of your trousers dirty.

They did go — dance halls, cheap lodging houses, gang turf. This body of work became known as the Chicago School, and its working intuition was a good one: a city is not a pile of buildings, it is a machine that assigns people to positions. In the diagram of concentric zones Ernest Burgess drew in 1925, the ring pressed against the business district is the zone of transition — cheapest, most crowded, highest turnover.

A bit earlier, the German sociologist Georg Simmel had written in 1903 that the city dweller grows a blasé attitude: too much stimulus, the nerves cannot react to every item, so differences get flattened — not coldness, exhausted bandwidth. In 1938 Louis Wirth boiled the tradition down to three variables: size, density, heterogeneity. The more people, the more crowded, the more unlike one another, the more relations among acquaintances give way to relations conducted by function.

The trouble was their next sentence: they judged those crowded poor districts in the zone of transition to be "socially disorganised" — no norms, no organisation, loose sand.

The man who dragged that sentence across the floor was in his twenties. From 1937 to 1940, William Foote Whyte moved into an Italian-American neighbourhood in Boston's North End for three and a half years (he called it Cornerville in the book), spending his days with a man he called Doc and his corner-boy crew. He found the order there terrifyingly fine-grained: who should pay, who speaks first, who owes whom a favour, all of it ranked and stable.

The finest stroke is bowling. Whyte wrote the scores down game by game and found that how well a man bowled tracked his rank in the group — a low-ranking man who bowled well elsewhere simply could not deliver against his seniors; the others jeered, and he deflated. Same pair of hands, different setting, different score. Status is not an abstraction; it bent the ball on the spot.

The transferable point: "there is no order here" very often means "wrong instrument for seeing order" — the observer walks in carrying a picture of what order looks like, fails to find it, and reports its absence. (Herbert Gans reached the same conclusion in another district of the same city in 1962 and called his book The Urban Villagers.) The disorganisation vocabulary took heavy criticism later, and deserved it: it read a group's way of living as pathology. Evidence: the concentric-zone model is descriptive induction from a single city; Whyte and Gans are single-site long-term fieldwork, giving mechanism rather than distribution.

// 02

Two streets apart, children grow up to earn differently — the hard part is proving the neighbourhood did it

Within one city, life expectancy can differ by more than a decade between postcodes. Anyone can measure the gap; the hard step is the next one. Did the neighbourhood produce these outcomes, or were people whose trajectories already differed sorted into different neighbourhoods? This is not pedantry: where you live is never random — income, work, where your relatives are, whether you can get a loan, all take part in that sorting. So "children from poor neighbourhoods end up poor" proves nothing at all.

One route is to measure the property of the neighbourhood itself. In 1995 Robert Sampson, Stephen Raudenbush and Felton Earls surveyed 8,782 residents across 343 Chicago neighbourhood clusters. What they measured is called collective efficacy: whether neighbours trust one another, plus whether they are willing to act for the common good — if a few adolescents are hanging around the street during school hours, will anyone say something.

The result appeared in Science in 1997: violence was markedly lower in neighbourhoods with high collective efficacy, and the association held after controlling for poverty, residential stability, individual characteristics and prior levels of violence. But this is correlational — low efficacy could equally be the consequence of violence rather than its cause.

The second route is far harder: randomise directly. From 1994 to 1998 the US Department of Housing and Urban Development ran an experiment called Moving to Opportunity, enrolling 4,604 families living in high-poverty public housing across five cities and assigning them at random, one group receiving a voucher usable only in low-poverty neighbourhoods. That is rare in social science — "where you live" was actually randomised.

The early results disappointed a lot of people: in 2007 Kling, Liebman and Katz reported that adult employment and earnings did not improve. The turn came in 2016, when Chetty, Hendren and Katz linked the experimental roster to tax records and looked again once the children had reached their twenties. Those who moved before turning 13 were earning about $3,477 more per year in their mid-twenties than the control group (a control mean of $11,270, so roughly 31% higher), and attended college at higher rates; those who moved after 13 gained nothing, and slightly less than nothing.

That changed the shape of the question. A neighbourhood is not an on/off switch but more like a dose accumulating by the year: the longer you live there the more accrues, and a move in your teens leaves too few years to accrue. The early studies saw no effect because they looked too soon — and at the wrong people.

Two things stated honestly. The sample is families who volunteered to move out of high-poverty public housing, so extrapolate with care. More importantly: we know it works without knowing what it works through. School? Peers? Safety? Still unsettled — and different answers imply completely different policies.

// 03

Rents rose and the original residents were mostly not driven out — here the statistics fight the intuition

In 1964 Ruth Glass, a sociologist working in London, coined a word for what she was watching: rows of working-class housing bought up one by one by the middle class, refurbished, and the social composition of whole districts replaced along with them. She called it gentrification. What drives it has had two explanations coexisting ever since.

One looks at capital: Neil Smith's rent gap, proposed in 1979 — when the ground rent a decayed district currently earns falls far enough below what it could earn, and the gap grows large enough, capital flows in to refurbish and capture the difference; who moves in is consequence, not cause. The other looks at people: David Ley's account is demand-side, a post-war middle class working in culture and knowledge whose tastes ran to old housing and walkable streets, and whose preferences pulled the capital after them. Both sides have presentable evidence and neither has beaten the other — the dispute is over weights, not over existence, and this page passes no judgement between them.

The counterintuitive part is one layer down. Intuitively, gentrification equals original residents being pushed out. But since the 2000s a body of work that tracks specific individuals has produced a different result: Freeman using national housing survey data, Ding and colleagues using a Philadelphia credit-bureau panel, Brummet and Reed using longitudinal census data — low-income residents of gentrifying neighbourhoods generally do not move out at higher rates than those in comparable non-gentrifying ones.

Don't conclude yet: the same body of work also found that the change in a district's composition comes mainly from the inflow side — college-educated people move in at a much higher rate.

Schematic, not actual data. An out-migration rate sees only the people who left. Who takes the vacated homes is something it cannot measure at all.

Put those two findings together and the out-migration rate is aimed at only half the picture. Homes empty all the time — a job change, a marriage, a return home. What decides whether the district changes is who takes them next: if everyone who takes them can afford the new price, the next generation of the original residents can never get back in. Peter Marcuse gave this situation a name back in the 1980s: exclusionary displacement — the people kept out never appear on any out-migration list, because they never arrived.

So the disagreement here is first of all about aperture, not about sides: one measure counts the outflow, the other would have to count an inflow that would otherwise have happened. The same body of work also found that the low-income residents who do leave land in worse places, and that less-educated renters are hit harder. "The overall out-migration rate is not high" and "some people were hit very hard" can both be true at once.

Evidence strength: these are administrative data or credit panels able to follow the same individuals, considerably better than cross-sections, but none is a randomised experiment, and results vary a great deal between cities. Whether anything should be done about it is a value judgement, outside the range of this discipline — what can be stated clearly here is only this: which arrow is it that you mean to change.

🎒 In Practice · BigCat

  • ① Investing and position sizing——every year when you review a strategy, you pull "the past ten years of constituents" of some index, run the historical prices through, and the result always looks better than what you actually traded. This runs the aperture mechanism from "rents rose and the original residents were mostly not driven out": the list in your hand is a survivors' list, and the names that were in the index back then and were later dropped or delisted have vanished cleanly from the data — exactly as an out-migration rate cannot see the people who never managed to move in. What to change: rerun the same rules against the constituent list as it stood on each date; the gap between the two curves is what your review has been overstating all along. What to stop: using an off-the-shelf "historical data for the constituents of index X" as your sample pool.
  • ② Parenting and education——the decision that surfaces every few years: whether to move house, change schools, or take a posting abroad for the sake of the environment. What is on the table is usually "how much better is it over there". This runs the mechanism from "two streets apart": in Moving to Opportunity the very same move produced completely different outcomes before and after age 13 — what does the work is the number of years of exposure. What to change: make the decision variable "how much better × how many years are left to live there" rather than "how much better". What to stop: deferring with "we can adjust later" — deferring is itself shaving that multiplier, and shaving the most valuable years of it.

🌀 Crossing Over · Connections Beyond the Field

  • Physics and mathematics · everyone is happy to live in a mixed neighbourhood, and everyone ends up living in a single-colour one: in 1971 Thomas Schelling laid out a grid on paper, put down two kinds of counters, and gave each counter one very mild rule: if too few of my neighbours are like me, move to the nearest empty square where that isn't so. "Too few" can be set very loosely — loosely enough that every counter is perfectly happy in a mixed setting. Iterate anyway and the board splits into large blocks of single colour. Physicists later matched the thing up with the model of spin alignment in magnets, which brought the whole apparatus of phase transitions and critical thresholds with it. This runs the mechanism from "two streets apart": neighbourhood composition is an intermediate layer, and individual choices are not summed directly but first rewrite that layer, which then rewrites the next round of choices. Which yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: the degree of segregation you observe does not imply the strength of individual preferences — "segregation this severe shows how deep the aversion runs" is an invalid inference, because mild and strong preferences can draw the same picture. Where it fails: on the grid, moving is free, an empty square waits for you, and every counter follows the same rule. In a real city moving costs money, the available housing has already been filtered by price and credit, and who could get a loan on which streets has a specific institutional history. So what the model proves is that strong preferences are not required to produce segregation, not that segregation comes only from preferences — reading the first as the second is its most common misuse.
  • Evolutionary biology · larger animals are cheaper to run, larger cities are costlier, and the two ledgers must be kept apart: biology has a very stable empirical regularity, Kleiber's law — double an animal's body mass and resting metabolic rate rises only about 68% (mass to the three-quarter power). So an elephant is cheaper per kilogram than a mouse, by sharing one distribution network that spreads the cost. In 2007 the group around Bettencourt and West carried the same fitting over to cities: total lane miles of road in US metropolitan areas scale with population at an exponent of about 0.85, sublinear, in the same direction as biology. But the socioeconomic side flips over — patents, wages and economic output scale with exponents generally above 1, so the more people, the more output per head. This runs Wirth's density variable from "someone moved into a 'chaotic' district": density directly raises the number of encounters per unit time. Which yields a conclusion you could not otherwise see: "bigger cities are more efficient" means two different things for infrastructure and for output, and has to be measured separately; and the encounter-rate half takes the bad with the good — the same superlinear exponents turn up in crime and epidemic data, so you cannot credit the city for innovation while blaming individuals for crime. Where it fails: larger organisms run slower and larger cities run faster, and that reversal of sign shows a city is not a scaled-up organism — the shared-network story only accounts for the sublinear half. These exponents are also quite sensitive to where a city's boundary is drawn: fit them to administrative borders or to commuting zones and the numbers shift.

Going Deeper

If a district's buildings, roads and shops all stayed put and only the residents were swapped out wholesale, would the crime rate travel with the people?
This is the extreme version of "is collective efficacy cause or effect". Moving to Opportunity moved people rather than neighbourhoods, so it cannot answer it; you would need a natural experiment in which the population turns over wholesale while the physical environment holds still. The trap: a wholesale change of residents usually comes with a change of management and policing too, and then what you measured is not the people.
What could ever show the concept of collective efficacy to be wrong?
Its problem is being too useful: whichever district is quiet gets called high-efficacy. To make it checkable, the measurement has to be independent of the outcome — score the efficacy first, then predict subsequent changes in violence, rather than explaining what has already happened after the fact.
All of this was worked out in cities with net inward migration. Does the same machinery run in a city that is losing people and emptying out?
Doubtful. The rent gap presupposes that the potential ground rent will be realised, and with net outflow that premise is gone: the same decayed district may simply keep decaying with nobody arriving to refurbish. Collective efficacy, conversely, may carry more weight — with fewer people left, the difference between willing and unwilling to step in gets amplified. Every number on this page comes from a handful of cities, so extend across cultures with real care.
