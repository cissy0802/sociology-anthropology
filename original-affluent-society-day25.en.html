DAY 25 · THE ORIGINAL AFFLUENT SOCIETY

The Original Affluent Society

The counter-intuitive line of the day: affluence is the ratio of means to wants, not the absolute quantity of things on hand — make the denominator small enough and someone with almost no property counts as rich.

2026-09-18 · BigCat
How many hours a day does someone living by hunting and gathering actually spend on food? Nobody followed them around with a notebook until the middle of the last century. The answer was four or five.

You have probably heard the storyline: people once worked dawn to dusk simply to stay alive, then agriculture arrived with surpluses, and only then could anyone be idle, and only then came writing, temples and civilisation. The line is so smooth that almost nobody went back and checked its first sentence.

In the spring of 1966 a conference on hunting and gathering peoples met in Chicago. Someone spread out a stack of time budgets — not estimates, but records kept day by day by observers who followed people around. This page is about those numbers, about how much of them was later revised away, and about a more awkward question: whether arranging the human past as a single line running from hardship to comfort holds up at all.

// 01

Four or five hours of work a day — and for the rest of it, what the anthropologists recorded was sleeping and talking

In 1948 an Australian–American expedition reached Arnhem Land in northern Australia. McCarthy and McArthur did something crude and durable: they timed people with a notebook — who left camp for food when, and when they came back. The 1960 figures: men spent about five hours a day on the food quest, women about six and a half including processing, and with travel deducted both fall to three or four. Calories averaged a little over two thousand a day per person, adequate by the nutritional standards then in use. A few years later Richard B. Lee spent four weeks recording the same thing among the Ju/'hoansi of the Dobe area in Botswana — in the earlier literature they appear as the !Kung — and got twelve to nineteen hours a week obtaining food.

Marshall Sahlins turned these numbers into a concept at that conference, and what he really changed was a definition: affluence means wants satisfied, and that is a ratio — means divided by wants. So there are two roads to it. Enlarge the numerator by producing more, which is the road industrial society took; or shrink the denominator, wanting little and having it within reach. Foragers took the second road, which is what makes them the original affluent society. The essay became the first chapter of his 1972 Stone Age Economics.

What Sahlins moved was the definition: affluence is a ratio, so there are two ways to change it.

The revisions cut deeper than most people realise. Lee's accounting covered only the food quest, leaving out processing, tool maintenance, housework and childcare. When he later added them in, the total work week rose to around forty hours — on a par with industrial labour. The samples were small too: two camps, four weeks.

The more fundamental blow is called the Kalahari debate. From the late 1980s Wilmsen and others argued that the Ju/'hoansi were never isolated survivors of the Pleistocene: they had long traded and intermarried with Bantu-speaking herders and farmers, had been drawn into colonial and market relations, and lived as they did because they had been pushed onto the poorest land. Lee's side does not accept the reading, and the argument has never fully closed.

One more thing cannot be skipped: short hours are not a good life. Infant mortality, life expectancy and rates of violent death in these societies are not numbers anyone today would accept. Evidence strength: small samples, short periods, contested accounting, substantially trimmed by later revision. What does survive on wider evidence is that farming brings neither leisure nor health automatically — skeletal samples from many regions show falling stature and rising rates of caries and enamel hypoplasia, the horizontal bands teeth carry when stress hits during their development. The size of the effect varies by region; the direction does not.

// 02

It is not that they had more things — they wanted fewer, and on moving day the more you own the worse it gets

In 1969 Lee published a very short piece about the Christmas he spent in the Kalahari. Wanting to thank his hosts, he bought the largest ox he could find. Word spread and people came one after another to tell him the animal was skin and bone and everyone would go hungry. He spent weeks embarrassed. The ox was butchered and turned out thick with fat. A Ju/'hoansi man later explained the practice: belittling the big animal someone brings is something they do all the time, so that whoever made the kill cannot start lifting his chin. This is not politeness. It is an institution everyone knows they are operating.

The same system contains a still neater rule: who distributes the meat depends not on who shot the animal but on who owns the arrow — and arrows are among the things people most often give each other. So skill at hunting does not convert into standing; the glory of the distribution walks off with a borrowed arrow. They also run an exchange system called hxaro, in which each adult keeps ten or twenty standing partners, many of them a hundred kilometres away; gifts are never returned at once and equivalence is not sought. The piece on gifts and reciprocity covered why giving binds more tightly than trading. This is the other end of the same mechanism — things that keep moving cannot settle on anyone.

James Woodburn's 1982 paper on egalitarian societies gave all of this a sharper frame: immediate-return and delayed-return systems. In an immediate-return system what is obtained today is eaten today, nothing is stored, no long-binding investment is made, and so nobody can make others dependent by controlling a stock. In a delayed-return system labour goes in now and the yield comes months or even years later, and holding that interval open requires property, obligation, and rights over things and over people. Woodburn himself noted the Hadza as an intermediate case.

The step most easily misread: equality here is not a matter of these people being kind-hearted. It is a running apparatus with real costs — insulting the meat, the arrow rule, the ceaseless circulation of gifts — each of which dismantles the possibility of accumulation. And once accumulating becomes cheap — storable grain, fixed plots, water you can fence — the levelling apparatus stops holding. Evidence strength: an ethnographic typology rather than a causal test; its predictive record is decent, since archaeologically the places where marked wealth differences first appear nearly always had storable, ownable things first.

So the contemporary lesson is not that owning less sets you free. It is that whether accumulation happens is priced by external conditions. The harder something is to store, to transfer, or to defer past the moment of use, the less stable domination grows around it. That is a description of conditions, not a recommendation about which way to go.

// 03

The same people may live under one set of rules in winter and another in summer

In 1904–05 Marcel Mauss and Henri Beuchat studied how Inuit social form varied with the seasons. The facts are clean: in winter people gathered into house complexes, sharing space and food with a dense ritual life; in summer they dispersed into small families under tents, each going their own way. Their conclusion was that these societies held two social structures within a single year, and two systems of law and religion along with them.

The North American Plains give a harder case. During the summer communal bison hunt, particular societies exercised coercive power — they could confiscate property and punish anyone who broke ranks and startled the herd, very much like a police force. When the hunt ended the power dissolved on the spot, to be reconstituted the following year.

Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything (2021) pushed this kind of material to the front: if the same people change political form twice a year, then asking what developmental stage a society is at starts to fail. What you are looking at is not a stage but a group choosing different arrangements by season, and knowing that they are choosing.

Archaeology supplies two hard stones. Göbekli Tepe in south-eastern Turkey, where huge T-shaped pillars enclose monumental structures, dates to roughly 9600–8200 BCE, and no domesticated plants or animals have been recovered there — the builders were not yet farming. Poverty Point in Louisiana, about 3700 to 3100 years old, is a set of concentric earthen ridges and five mounds raised by people living from fishing, hunting and gathering. Agriculture itself does not look like a revolution either: plant remains in the Near East show domestication traits accumulating little by little over two or three thousand years.

The criticism belongs here in full. The book is an interpretive synthesis of existing material, not new data, and reviewers have charged it with selective use of evidence and with reading the seasonality material too hard. One reverse reading deserves to be put down as it stands: if people were already accepting coercive authority in certain seasons this early, the same evidence can be read as showing that humans adapted to hierarchy earlier than assumed — the opposite of the authors' conclusion. Evidence strength: interpretive synthesis — the individual cases are solid, the overall argument is contested.

What travels is not that the past was better. It is something colder: the real cost of arranging the human past in a single line is not that the order comes out wrong, but that it makes other ways of doing things unthinkable — anything off the line gets filed as not yet arrived. The Boasian revolution discussed in the piece on cultural relativism was against exactly this.

🎒 Scenarios · BigCat

  • ① Investing and position sizing—every raise or bonus feels like something for two months, and from the third month monthly spending has quietly absorbed the increment; the savings rate is back at its old number while the balance keeps climbing, so it always feels as though things are improving. This runs the mechanism from the section on four or five hours of work a day: the numerator rises and the denominator rises with it, leaving the ratio where it was. What to change: switch the number you watch from the balance to the savings rate; stop the habit of spending a month to see how it feels, and have the increment swept out automatically in the month the raise lands.
  • ② Engineering and system design—a service has been running three or four years and nobody dares touch it, because its state is scattered across a local disk, a few hand-edited tables and a cron job on one machine, and every attempt to break it up stalls on the data not being movable. This runs the mechanism from the section on wanting fewer things and moving day: whether things settle is priced by the cost of moving, and once moving is expensive, settling is guaranteed and then locks the structure in place. What to change: give every new service one hard rule — state may only land somewhere it can be exported wholesale; and treat "when was this last rebuilt from scratch" as a number to look at regularly, rather than only watching whether it has gone down.

🌀 Crossing over · Connections across disciplines

  • Evolutionary ecology · when a tit leaves a berry patch can be calculated from a formula, and the formula contains no term for being full: foraging ecology has a result called the marginal value theorem (Charnov 1976): how long an animal stays in a food patch depends on when that patch's instantaneous return rate drops below the average return rate for the habitat as a whole — when it drops, the animal leaves, and how hungry it is has little to do with it. This runs the mechanism from the section on four or five hours of work a day: the stopping point is not an absolute quantity (enough food for today) but a threshold (continuing is worse than going back). From which follows a conclusion you could not otherwise see: in economies of this kind the length of the working day cannot be used as a measure of living standards — as environment and tools improve, hours may stay flat or even fall, because patch quality and threshold move together; two societies with identical hours can differ enormously in how well off they are. But it does not hold here: the theorem assumes the yield cannot be stored and is consumed on the spot. Once storage is possible, gathering beyond present need acquires value for the first time and the stopping point is no longer set by the current return rate — which is precisely the moment a delayed-return system appears. So the theorem has almost no predictive power for working hours in agricultural societies.
  • Neuroscience · you walk indoors out of bright sun and a few minutes later you can see again, though the room has not got any brighter: the retina never encodes absolute light intensity; it encodes departure from a current baseline, and that baseline shifts wholesale with the ambient average. This is what lets hardware with a limited dynamic range cover illumination spanning ten orders of magnitude. This runs the mechanism from the section on wanting fewer things: satisfaction depends on a ratio, and the denominator follows the numerator. From which follows a conclusion you could not otherwise see: any welfare measure defined on absolute levels will decouple from subjective satisfaction over time, and this is not human insatiability but the necessary result of a shifting baseline; so to predict discontent in a population, look at rates of change and reference groups rather than at levels — two populations at the same level, one slowly improving and one just fallen from somewhere higher, will differ completely. But it does not hold here: adaptation has a hard floor, and below a certain number of photons no amount of adapting restores sight. Correspondingly, absolute deprivations — calories, disease, cold — do not move with the reference point, so the shrink-your-wants road fails outright on those dimensions. That is exactly where original affluence is least defensible: slack in the time budget buys no slack in infant mortality.

Going deeper

If foraging was so affluent, why did almost everyone eventually switch to farming?
There is no settled answer, but several threads hold up. Agriculture supports population densities one or two orders of magnitude higher, so even if the average person is worse off, farming populations outnumber foraging ones — what gets selected is group size, not individual welfare. And there is a very practical thread: once the neighbours start storing grain, not storing makes you the one who gets raided. A testable marker: if farming were adopted because it made people better off, early farmers' skeletons should be healthier. Most samples show the reverse.
Does "shrink your wants" really count as a road?
As a social mechanism it does: where a community has levelling devices — insulting the meat, gifts that must keep moving, nobody permitted to leverage a stock over others — wanting more carries a social cost in itself. But it is the product of a collective apparatus, not of personal cultivation: one person suppressing wants by will and a whole community doing it together differ by orders of magnitude in difficulty. And the road is useless against absolute scarcity — too few calories is too few calories.
Which resources most readily grow inequality around them?
On Woodburn's distinction: the ones that can be stored, whose access a few people can hold, and where the gap between input and return is long. Take a resource near you and ask one question — will it spoil if it goes unused today? If the answer is no, property and dependence will grow around it sooner or later; if yes, the rules around it tend to look more like sharing. Non-material things can be tested the same way, an example being information inside a team.
