DAY 12 · CULTURE AND TASTE

Bourdieu

The most counterintuitive line here: the most informative part of a person's taste is not what they like, but what strikes them as trying too hard

2026-09-05 · BigCat
The most expensive part of an education charges no tuition and issues no certificate. It happens at the dinner table, in other people's living rooms, and at the age of three, listening to how the adults talk to a doctor.

The first time you walk into an unfamiliar setting — an expensive restaurant, someone else's company meeting room, a family dinner where everyone knows everyone but you — nobody hands you a manual, yet within seconds you have adjusted: how fast you walk, how loud you talk, where your hands go, when you laugh. Afterwards you could not say which rule you were following.

This page takes apart three things: how that unsayable equipment gets loaded into a body; how institutions like schools and companies read it back as "personal quality"; and why, when it comes to taste, what counts as overdone locates a person better than what counts as good.

// 01

You cannot state the rule, yet you know how to walk the moment you enter

Pierre Bourdieu started not from theory but from two stretches of fieldwork. In the late 1950s he did anthropological research in the Kabylia highlands of Algeria; back in France in the early 1960s he returned to Béarn, the rural south-west where he grew up, to work out something the locals themselves found puzzling: eldest sons could not find wives.

By local inheritance custom the farm went to the eldest son, which by that same custom should have made him the best available match. But urbanisation accelerated through the 1950s, young women increasingly preferred to marry into town, and the men the rules favoured most were the ones left single in large numbers. What Bourdieu noticed was this: if "the rules" explained conduct, that outcome could not have arisen.

What actually operated was another layer — which match was thinkable, who at a village dance dared ask whom, what a farmer's way of walking and standing meant to a girl raised in town. Not one of these was written down anywhere, and together they decided who married. His 1962 article on celibacy in Béarn was the first place he used the word habitus, explicitly borrowing from the anthropologist Mauss and his essay on techniques of the body: even how you walk is learned.

Habitus is not a list of behaviours but a generator: a system of embodied dispositions laid down by a durable position — posture, accent, intuitions about what is worth wanting, a sense of what feels awkward. Its key property is that it is generative: in a situation you have never met, it still improvises a response for you. Which is why it is harder to change than a rule — a rule can be memorised, a disposition can only be trained. It also explains a familiar embarrassment: you know perfectly well what to do, and your body is still half a beat late.

The standard objection to all this is a serious one: the concept risks circularity. Any behaviour whatsoever can be labelled habitus after the fact, which means it forbids nothing and cannot be falsified; and it is extremely hard to measure. State the evidence plainly: habitus is a concept grown out of ethnographic material, not a testable prediction. Its value lies in making visible that a batch of differences usually filed under "personal style" have a systematic source.

// 02

School grades you with a ruler it does not itself teach

At a parents' evening a teacher says a child "expresses herself well". Which class taught that? There is no such line on the report card, yet it enters the assessment.

Bourdieu and Jean-Claude Passeron, in Les Héritiers (1964) and La Reproduction (1970), aimed at exactly that gap: French higher education admitted wildly unequal proportions by social origin while the school sincerely believed it was selecting fairly. Their claim is not that schools cheat. It is that part of what a school rewards is not in its curriculum — that part is picked up at home, tuition-free but far from cost-free (the cost being time, books on shelves, the way adults talk). Sociology calls this cultural capital. The "cultural fit" seen in elite hiring in the social stratification topic is the same machine at the workplace end.

Bourdieu split it into three states, and the split is more useful than the term. Embodied: accent, bearing, a feel for which topic is appropriate — only absorbed over years. Objectified: the books, instruments and pictures in a house — purchasable, though owning is not the same as being able to use. Institutionalised: the credential. That third step is the decisive leap: a diploma converts "my family had these things" into "I hold this qualification", and the origin is severed at the moment of exchange.

Can any of it be measured? Paul DiMaggio tried in the American Sociological Review in 1982, using eleventh-graders in the 1960 Project Talent survey and factor-analysing responses like wanting to attend classical concerts, reading serious literature, and thinking of oneself as cultivated. Net of ability test scores and family status, the measure still predicted school grades, with different effects for boys and girls. Evidence: one cross-sectional correlation on 1960 US data; replications elsewhere find the direction more often than not but magnitudes vary widely — this is not a firmly replicated law.

More vivid is Annette Lareau's Unequal Childhoods (2003). She and her assistants interviewed 88 children and their parents, and followed 12 families closely. One of the sharpest settings she records is the paediatrician's office: middle-class children, coached by their parents beforehand, ask the doctor questions and even correct him; working-class and poor children are taught to be quiet and respectful in front of authority. She names the two styles concerted cultivation and the accomplishment of natural growth.

One of her points is routinely dropped by people citing her: the second is better for children in many respects — far more free play, closer ties to relatives. Its disadvantage is not in the child; it is that institutions like schools and hospitals do not cash it. Evidence: small-sample ethnography, mechanism clearly visible, proportions not to be extrapolated.

// 03

The most informative part of a taste is what strikes you as trying too hard

Someone says a thing is "a bit tacky", or "trying too hard". You can more or less feel what they mean, and nobody can state the standard. Bourdieu's La Distinction (1979, translated 1984) is an entire book taking that feeling apart.

The data are ordinary survey data: two rounds, in 1963 and 1967–68, 1,217 people in all, asked about music, painting, photography, furniture, food and dress. Preferences map systematically onto educational capital and parental occupation. What grows in permanently straitened circumstances he calls the taste of necessity — substantial, sufficient, function first, a photograph should show what it is a photograph of — while at the high-education pole attention goes to form rather than content, and one can talk at length about a picture with "nothing in it".

His most counterintuitive claim is about mechanism: taste operates mainly by refusal. Aesthetic opposition is social opposition first — saying "too sweet", "too flashy" draws a line between yourself and someone. So ask a person what they like and the answer is often vague; ask what they cannot stand and their position is immediately legible. And which taste counts as legitimate is not a natural growth: it is underwritten by schools, museums and critics. The front-stage performance in the dramaturgy topic is a deliberate management of impressions; this layer is different — what strikes you as overdone is not something you perform, it is something you cannot help.

Income is one axis; Bourdieu wants two. Vertical is total capital, horizontal is its composition — weighted toward the cultural or the economic. What one axis cannot say: two groups at the same height (professors and business owners) tend to have systematically opposite preferences in music, furniture and holidays, and to look down on each other. The two points on the dashed line are indistinguishable on a one-dimensional ladder.

How much of this still holds? The most important revision comes from Richard Peterson and Roger Kern in 1996: comparing the 1982 and 1992 rounds of the American Survey of Public Participation in the Arts, they found high-status people no longer consuming only highbrow culture but ranging visibly wider, a change driven both by cohort replacement and by change within the same people. They call the type a cultural omnivore. This does not overturn distinction; it changes its form: the marker moves from what you listen to toward how many kinds and in what stance — listening to everything and speaking well about each has itself become the scarce thing.

The strongest counter-position deserves stating as its authors would. John Goldthorpe's 2007 critique makes two points: cultural capital is loosely formalised with a weak causal chain, and educational inequality can be explained without it at all. The relative risk aversion model he proposed with Richard Breen in 1997 assumes only one thing — that at every educational branching point, families of every class aim above all to avoid downward mobility, and since "downward" means a different distance from different starting points, that asymmetry alone generates class-differentiated choices. The two have been compared head to head: a 2007 study of Amsterdam secondary students concluded that they govern different links in the chain — cultural capital more closely tied to school performance, relative risk aversion to which track is chosen at equal performance. Evidence: a single-city sample, with results inconsistent across countries. The dispute has not converged; neither side should be taken as settled.

🎒 In practice · BigCat

  • ① Leading teams and organisations — the review that confirms a new hire, where the write-ups always contain "communicates well" or "fitted in fast". This runs the mechanism from "School grades you with a ruler it does not itself teach": the institution reads familiarity with unwritten local norms as personal quality, and those norms were never written down. What to change: strike those phrases from the form and allow only specific behaviour (which design review, which risk named, whose document got reused). What to watch is how many people per year land in "high write-up, flat output six months later" and in the reverse — that mismatch rate is what tells you what the ruler was measuring.
  • ② Parenting and education — the weekend each term spent picking after-school classes, where every option on the table leads to a grade or a certificate. The same mechanism from the other end: the part that institutions actually cash — putting your own question clearly to an unfamiliar adult — is precisely the part no exam grades, so it never appears on a sign-up form. What to change: keep the classes, but move "talking to the teacher, the doctor, the coach" from the parent doing it to the child speaking first. (Lareau's own caution: this does not mean concerted cultivation is better, only that institutions cash it.)

🌀 Crossing over · Interdisciplinary echoes

  • Neuroscience and consciousness · "Cannot say it" is not a figure of speech but a division of labour between two memory systems: riding a bike, the grammar of your first language, touch typing — all can be done and none can be recited. Neuroscience distinguishes declarative memory (facts you can state) from procedural memory (skills you can perform), and they depend on different brain structures; the most famous evidence comes from patients with severe hippocampal damage who cannot remember what happened yesterday yet still improve at a new motor skill day after day, with no memory of practising. This runs the mechanism from "You cannot state the rule, yet you know how to walk the moment you enter": habitus is a trained disposition, not a memorised rule, and can only be acquired through repeated exposure. From which follows a conclusion not otherwise visible: explicit knowledge and implicit disposition are acquired at speeds an order of magnitude apart, the first crammable and the second not, so any screening that reads both signals will have the crammable part flattened by coaching, leaving all remaining discriminating power in the part that cannot be taught. Hence a perverse pattern: the more standardised and widely available the training for a skill, the less weight that skill carries in selection. But it does not hold here: procedural learning by itself carries no status — however well you ride a bike, there is no social premium. The brain level can establish that some things can only be trained; it cannot say why a small subset of them (accent, how one talks at a table) is assigned value while others are not. That valuation is entirely institutional, and it is not in the head.
  • Distributed systems and AI · A system that never collects origins will reconstruct them anyway: the classic recommender technique is collaborative filtering — no understanding of the content, only "people whose behaviour resembles yours also consumed this". This runs the mechanism from "The most informative part of a taste is what strikes you as trying too hard": taste is a function of position, so taste can be run backwards to infer position. From which follows a conclusion not otherwise visible: a system fed only behavioural data, collecting no demographic attribute at all, will still grow internal partitions that overlap heavily with class — the classification is already encoded in the preferences and does not need to be supplied. Landing on an observable consequence: when evaluating such a system, "we collect no sensitive fields" is not evidence of anything; the only evidence is how far its outputs diverge once grouped by external population data. But it does not hold here: in Bourdieu's account tastes are ranked, and the ranking is underwritten by schools, museums and critics. Collaborative filtering has no legitimating institution, only co-occurrence counts — it can sort people into non-overlapping clusters but cannot produce the category "vulgar". So it reproduces separation without producing a hierarchy of contempt; the contempt on a platform is what users bring in, not a product of the algorithm's structure.

Questions worth sitting with

If everyone read the same books and took the same classes, would distinction disappear?
Distinction runs on difference, not on any particular content. Equalising supply only relocates the marker — from whether you have read it to how you read it, and whether you look like you are working at it. So do not watch whether the distribution of content changed; watch which signal took over in the settings where people get screened.
If habitus can explain anything, is it still a useful concept?
That is the hardest objection to it, and there is no way around it. The only thing that can rescue it is naming what it forbids. It does offer one: people whose position changes abruptly go through a stretch of being out of step — old dispositions misfiring in new settings, the moves still there but each one half a beat off (Bourdieu called it hysteresis). That can be observed, and it can fail. A concept is worth what it dares to exclude.
Is "the taste of necessity" itself already a view from above?
This is one of the criticisms Bourdieu has taken: writing working-class culture as forced and deficient rather than as having standards of its own. The reflexivity discussed in the fieldwork and ethnography topic applies exactly here — when you describe another person's taste, the classification you use comes from a position too. The move is not to stop describing but to lay the ruler out: say who is doing the counting when something counts as necessary.
